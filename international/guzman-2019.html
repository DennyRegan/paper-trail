Guzmán, 2019: What the Jury Actually Found

In brief

The fourth superseding indictment against Joaquín Guzmán Loera carried seventeen counts and eighty-five alleged violations beneath its continuing criminal enterprise charge. What reached the jury was smaller: ten counts and twenty-seven proposed violations[S1][S8].

The jury convicted on all ten counts. It found twenty-five of the twenty-seven violations proved and rejected two — an alleged distribution of 19,000 kilograms of cocaine in March 2007, and 409 kilograms of marijuana in January 2012[S8]. It found that at least one proved violation involved 150 kilograms or more of cocaine, that the enterprise took at least $10 million in gross receipts in a twelve-month period, and that Guzmán was one of several principal administrators, organisers or leaders[S8].

The Second Circuit rejected all ten grounds of appeal in January 2022 and affirmed[S12].

Because the signed verdict sheet survives, what the jury decided can be stated exactly rather than summarised from the prosecution's account of it.

What happened

From indictment to trial

Mexico extradited Guzmán on 19 January 2017, after the Eastern District of New York returned a fourth superseding indictment in May 2016[S1][S13]. The Justice Department's announcement recorded six separate federal indictments nationwide; the government's detention memorandum names the other five districts[S2][S13]. The trial here arose from the Eastern District case alone[S12].

The indictment alleged that Guzmán and Ismael Zambada García conducted a continuing criminal enterprise from January 1989 to September 2014, with eighty-five proposed violations beneath that count. Counts Two to Four charged trafficking conspiracies, Counts Five to Fifteen particular international cocaine distributions, Count Sixteen firearms offences, and Count Seventeen money laundering[S1]. By trial the structure had changed: the verdict sheet carried ten counts and twenty-seven violations, and the Second Circuit expressly noted the numbering differed from the indictment[S8][S12].

Two categories of document need separating here. The government's pre-trial detention memorandum gave an expansive account of the organisation, the trafficking, the violence and the escapes — but it was a proffer written to support detention, and it said itself that it was not a complete statement of the evidence expected at trial[S2]. The motions in limine described evidence the prosecution wanted to use and asked for advance rulings[S4]. Judge Brian Cogan granted some, denied others, and deferred those where the proffers were insufficiently specific; he permitted designated witnesses to testify under pseudonyms while giving the defence their true identities and the relevant disclosure[S5].

Two pre-trial rulings mattered later. Cogan held that the murder conspiracy violation stated a substantive offence and refused to strike it, leaving particular objections for trial[S6]. He denied motions to suppress communications taken from servers in the Netherlands and data from FlexiSpy accounts on servers in the United States[S7]. Both were rulings about what could be charged or admitted, not findings that the conduct occurred.

The evidence

The Second Circuit described a three-month trial with testimony from fourteen cooperating witnesses[S12]. Reuters reported more than fifty witnesses overall, with former associates, a communications engineer, ledgers and an encrypted communications system forming much of the case, and more than a dozen former associates having struck cooperation deals[S17]. Cooperating witnesses testify under agreement with the government, which makes their evidence interested whatever weight a jury gives it.

The prosecution's verdict-day release listed its own inventory: fourteen cooperating witnesses, weapons, ledgers, messages, videos, photographs, recordings, and narcotics seizures it put at more than 130,000 kilograms of cocaine and heroin[S14]. That is the successful prosecution's public account of its evidence, not a jury finding. The DEA's release the same day belongs to the same government reporting stream and offers no independent corroboration, though it does not repeat the quantity figure[S14][S15].

The defence case, as Reuters reported, was that Guzmán had been made a fall guy while Zambada remained the more important figure[S17].

What the jury found

On 12 February 2019 the jury convicted on all ten counts[S8][S12].

Count One required unanimous agreement on at least three violations forming a continuing series. The verdict sheet records a decision on all twenty-seven, which is why the finding can be read in detail rather than as a single conclusion[S8].

Violations One to Twelve, twelve international cocaine distributions attributed on the form to dealings with the Norte del Valle Cartel: proved. Violations Thirteen to Fifteen, three distributions involving the Cifuentes-Villa organisation: proved. Violations Sixteen, Seventeen and Nineteen: proved. Violation Eighteen, the alleged distribution of 19,000 kilograms of cocaine in March 2007: not proved[S8].

In the domestic group, Violations Twenty to Twenty-Three, Twenty-Five and Twenty-Six: proved. Violation Twenty-Four, 409 kilograms of marijuana in January 2012: not proved. Violation Twenty-Seven, conspiracy to commit murder between 1989 and 2014: proved[S8].

Twenty-five proved, two rejected. The jury did not simply accept the form as presented to it.

Three further findings attached to Count One: at least one violation involving 150 kilograms or more of cocaine; at least $10 million in gross receipts in a twelve-month period; and Guzmán as one of several principal administrators, organisers or leaders[S8]. These are statutory thresholds and a status finding, not an aggregate quantity.

Count Two brought a guilty verdict on the international conspiracy, with findings of at least five kilograms of cocaine, one kilogram of heroin, 500 grams of methamphetamine and 1,000 kilograms of marijuana. Counts Three and Four, importation and distribution conspiracies, each carried the five-kilogram finding. Counts Five to Eight were substantive international distributions corresponding to particular Count One violations, guilty on each with at least five kilograms found. On Count Nine the jury found unlawful firearm use and answered that firearms had been brandished, discharged and included a machine gun. Count Ten, money laundering conspiracy: guilty[S8].

At sentencing the government moved to dismiss Counts Two to Four as lesser included offences. So ten counts drew guilty verdicts and seven survived as separately punished counts in the judgment. That imposed concurrent life terms on the enterprise count and the four substantive cocaine counts, a consecutive thirty-year term on firearms, a concurrent 240-month term on laundering, and forfeiture exceeding $12 billion — a judicial sentencing determination, separate from both the jury's minimum-quantity answers and the prosecution's seizure figure[S12].

The appeal

The Second Circuit took ten grounds and rejected all of them[S12]: the rule of speciality in the extradition treaty; pre-trial confinement and defence access; the sufficiency of the murder conspiracy predicate; the electronic searches; evidential rulings; an alleged conflict involving lead counsel; limits on a proposed government-bias defence; the unanimity instruction; juror misconduct; and alleged ex parte proceedings.

On extradition, the defence had argued before trial that the prosecution breached the treaty's rule of speciality, while acknowledging in its own filing that Second Circuit authority appeared to deny Guzmán standing to obtain dismissal on that ground[S3]. The court applied that authority, declined to revisit it, and recorded that Mexico had expressly consented under Article 17 of the treaty to the Eastern District prosecution[S12].

It also recorded Guzmán's allegation that the consent had been fraudulently obtained — and did not decide it. The claim failed on standing and on Mexico's express consent, so whether the alleged fraud occurred was never determined either way[S12].

It upheld the murder conspiracy ruling, and upheld the refusal to suppress: the Dutch calls fell outside Fourth Amendment protection in the circumstances, Guzmán had shown no protected expectation of privacy in the FlexiSpy data, and the Stored Communications Act warrant was not invalidated by the geographic restriction argued under Rule 41[S12].

The juror question

Eight days after the verdict, VICE published an interview with an unidentified juror alleging that jurors had followed media coverage against the court's instructions, discussed the case before deliberations began, and concealed some of that exposure when questioned. The reporter said he recognised the source from court, had reviewed detailed notes and had used jury-selection information to confirm the person's role — so what VICE could not independently corroborate was the substance of the allegations about other jurors, rather than whether its source had served[S16].

The defence sought an evidentiary hearing and a new trial on that basis; the government opposed[S9][S10].

Cogan's ruling answered two separate questions. He refused a hearing because the allegations did not meet the demanding threshold for recalling and questioning jurors. Then, on the new trial, he assumed the specific allegations were true, applied the hypothetical average juror test for prejudice, and denied relief anyway[S11]. The Second Circuit held that the unsworn and uncorroborated statements of one unidentified juror did not require further inquiry, and affirmed[S12].

Disputed and mythologised

Overstated

The jury found Guzmán responsible for 130,000 kilograms.

The figure is from the prosecution's verdict-day press release, describing seizures in evidence at trial[S14]. The verdict sheet contains no aggregate quantity finding at all. It found particular violations proved or not proved, found at least one involving 150 kilograms or more of cocaine, and made specified minimum-quantity findings on Counts Two to Eight[S8]. It also rejected an alleged 19,000-kilogram transaction outright[S8]. The 130,000 figure may fairly describe what the prosecution said its seizure evidence totalled. It is not what the jury decided.

Unproven

The juror allegations show the verdict was unsafe.

An anonymous juror told VICE that jurors followed coverage, talked early and concealed it; the account was unsworn and partly unverified by VICE itself[S16]. What happened next is more useful than the allegation. Cogan assumed the allegations true and still refused relief on an objective prejudice analysis[S11], and the Second Circuit held that one unidentified juror's uncorroborated statements did not require further inquiry[S12]. The courts did not find the allegations false. They found that, even taken at their highest, they did not disturb the verdict.

Overstated

Guzmán was a figurehead while others ran the organisation.

This was the defence position at closing, reported contemporaneously: a fall guy, with Zambada the more important continuing figure[S17]. The jury found Guzmán was one of several principal administrators, organisers or leaders[S8]. That finding is inconsistent with a powerless figurehead. It is equally not a finding that he sat at the top, or that nobody else held equal or greater authority — the statute required a status finding, not a ranking.

Unproven

The extradition was legally irregular.

The defence argued a breach of the treaty's rule of speciality and alleged defects in the later consent process, including that the consent had been fraudulently obtained[S3]. The Second Circuit applied existing authority on standing and recorded Mexico's express consent under Article 17[S12]. What it did not do is decide the fraud allegation: the claim failed for other reasons, so the question was left open rather than answered[S12]. The complete Mexican record is not in these sources — though the defence filing reproduces portions of the redacted consent material, identifies Diplomatic Note 17-0245 and describes the amparo decisions — so the Mexican side can be partly but not fully reconstructed here[S3][S12][S13].

Gaps in the record

  • The trial itself. No complete official transcript or exhibit archive was located among these sources, though the government's opposition quotes transcript passages and links particular witnesses, seizures and exhibits to particular violations. A partial reconstruction is possible; a witness-by-witness one is not[S8][S10][S12].
  • The cooperating witnesses. Fourteen testified[S12]. No complete cooperation agreements, examinations or jury instructions appear here, so a full assessment of credibility and corroboration is not possible from this set — although the material does identify cooperation incentives and describes documentary and seizure evidence offered as corroboration[S5][S10][S12][S17].
  • The Mexican side. The extradition decrees, amparo judgments, diplomatic notes and complete consent documentation are not among these sources in full[S3][S12][S13].
  • Provenance. The verdict sheet and several filings were opened as ECF-headed copies hosted on convictingchapo.com rather than through PACER or GovInfo; the new-trial motion and the government's opposition on Scribd; Cogan's Rule 33 decision through Courthouse News[S1][S3][S4][S5][S6][S7][S8][S9][S10][S11]. They carry court captions, docket numbers and filing details consistent with the appellate account, but they are not official repository copies. Official GovInfo copies exist for some of these rulings and should replace the third-party links where obtainable.
