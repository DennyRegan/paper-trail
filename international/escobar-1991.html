The Paper Trail

Escobar, 19 June 1991: The Surrender and La Catedral

In brief

Pablo Escobar surrendered to Colombian authorities on 19 June 1991, hours after the Constituent Assembly voted at first reading to prohibit the extradition of native-born Colombians[S13][S14][S20]. The Assembly voted again on 28 June, and the Constitution containing the prohibition was promulgated on 7 July, by which time he had been in custody for over a fortnight[S1][S4][S20].

A route into Colombian custody had existed since December 1990. Decreto 3030 set out a surrender procedure with sentence reductions attached, and Decreto 303 of 30 January 1991 broadened its protection so that a person who qualified would not be extradited for any offence committed before surrender, confessed or not, provided the sentence imposed was actually served[S2][S4]. That protection was conditional and confined to those who met the entry requirements. What the constitutional ban would add was a different order of guarantee, and it was not yet in force on the day he walked in.

The prison he entered was an unfinished municipal drug-rehabilitation centre in Envigado, taken on lease by the national government, which journalists were walked through before he arrived[S9][S11][S12]. Thirteen months later he escaped from it during a botched attempt to move him, and no court has determined the route he took[S7][S8][S17][S18].

What happened

The route in

Decreto 3030, issued on 14 December 1990 under emergency powers rather than by Congress, created a procedure by which a qualifying defendant could appear voluntarily before a Colombian judge and make a free and spontaneous confession capable of supporting a conviction. Confession earned a one-third reduction in sentence; specified cooperation and the disclosure of property could earn up to a further one-sixth[S2]. The decree restricted extradition while such a defendant was held in Colombian custody, and provided for that protection to be lost through escape or attempted escape, among other failures of compliance[S2].

The wider guarantee came six weeks later. Article 1 of Decreto 303, of 30 January 1991, extended the protection to any offence committed before the date of surrender, whether confessed or not, on condition that the custodial sentence imposed was actually served[S2][S4]. It reached only those who had first entered the Decreto 3030 procedure.

The official copy of Decreto 303 returns an error. Its Article 1 appears in the legislative-history notes attached to the official text of Decreto 3030, and is reproduced and analysed in a later judgment of the Constitutional Court, which is how its wording is established here[S2][S4].

What the decrees offered was proportional and conditional: reductions and protections available to a defined class of defendants. They named no sentence, no term of years and no prison.

19 June

The Constituent Assembly voted on 19 June 1991, by 51 votes to 13, to prohibit the extradition of Colombians by birth. Escobar surrendered the same day and was flown by government helicopter to the prison outside Envigado[S13][S14][S20]. He described the surrender publicly as an act of peace[S14].

The vote was not final. The Assembly voted a second time on 28 June, 45 to 5, and the Constitution took effect on promulgation on 7 July[S1][S4][S20]. The two occasions were not alike: the first was taken by secret ballot, save for delegates who chose to declare themselves, and the second by show of hands with Escobar already imprisoned[S20]. On the day he surrendered, the constitutional prohibition had passed one reading and had no legal force.

Extradition was among the most contested questions before the Assembly, and its treatment there followed years of conflict between the state and the Medellín organisation[S20]. The Washington Post reported that sources close to Escobar said he had waited for the vote despite already holding the government's surrender offer[S13].

That is evidence of what people close to him said, not of what he thought. The sequence is firm: the decree protection from January, the first Assembly vote and the surrender on 19 June, the second vote on 28 June, promulgation on 7 July[S1][S2][S4][S13][S14][S20]. Why he moved when he did is not established by the documents. What can be said is that a protection against extradition was already available to him under decree, subject to the surrender regime's conditions, and that the constitutional prohibition had not yet been enacted.

The legislative story continued after he was inside. Decreto 2265 of 4 October 1991 converted much of the surrender regime into permanent law but omitted the explicit non-extradition wording carried over from Decreto 303, a difference the Constitutional Court later examined directly[S4][S5].

The prison on paper

A fourteen-page government account released in July 1992, a week after the escape, and reported in detail by El Tiempo, sets out how the prison came to exist. The site was an unfinished drug-rehabilitation centre belonging to the municipality of Envigado. The national government took it on lease, the contract signed with the mayor on 30 May 1991[S9].

A memorandum of 29 May, described in the same account, set the intended policy. Responsibility for administration and security rested with national authority. Guards were to be selected by a committee comprising the mayor of Envigado, the prison director appointed by the Ministry of Justice, and the regional Procurador. Accommodation could be decent, but luxuries or exceptions that removed the institution's character as a prison were not to be tolerated[S9]. The same account records that Escobar's lawyer asked that police and military not enter the prison, that the government accepted the public forces would not enter the establishment, and that it denied creating any demilitarised zone[S9]. Further terms were reported separately[S10], but both reports draw on the same government material and are one stream of evidence rather than two.

Neither the signed lease nor the memorandum appears in the sources used here. What is established is what the government said about them a year later, in the week after a humiliating escape — important evidence, and also an institution explaining itself.

The prison as observed

Reporters were taken through La Catedral before Escobar entered it, which makes their accounts a different class of evidence from everything written afterwards. A Reuters dispatch of 10 June described builders converting the rehabilitation centre and recorded six large bedrooms with private bathrooms, a dormitory, kitchen, offices and a football pitch[S11]. Richard Boudreaux of the Los Angeles Times, writing on 14 June, described private bathrooms, a large suite, communal and games space and football facilities, with around 150 soldiers stationed outside the perimeter and police held farther back[S12].

Boudreaux also recorded the mayor of Envigado denying that Escobar owned or had financed the property, and saying the municipality had bought the land intending a rehabilitation centre[S12]. The Reuters account carries a different version: officials said the land had been seized from traffickers in 1989, while an unnamed army officer speculated it might once have been Escobar's[S11]. No deed, title or cadastral record appears in the sources used here.

What went wrong inside

Within two months the arrangement was publicly in trouble. In August 1991 justice officials resigned after disclosures about privileges granted to the prisoners, and the government acknowledged failures over visiting[S15].

The fullest official account of what was alleged survives in a Constitutional Court judgment of 1993, which reproduces the scope of the Procuraduría's disciplinary inquiry: unauthorised building works, excessive luxuries and improper alterations, administrative disorder, repeated breaches of discipline, excessive familiarity between guards and prisoners, weapons held by prisoners, and the escape[S6]. These are recorded as alleged irregularities under investigation. The judgment does not find that each was proved, and it itemises none of the amenities that later became famous.

In July 1992 came the deaths of Fernando Galeano and Gerardo Moncada. Contemporaneous reporting in the Washington Post, relying on anonymous law-enforcement sources, said both men had been summoned to La Catedral but were abducted after leaving it, their bodies recovered outside Medellín[S16]. A declassified DEA assessment written in 1994 states instead that members of the Moncada and Galeano families were summoned to the prison and killed there, without disclosing the basis for the statement[S19]. A Justice and Peace judgment of the Tribunal Superior de Medellín in 2014, in proceedings against a former paramilitary, records in its background section that Escobar summoned and killed both men while in custody; the passage carries no supporting footnote and forms no part of the charges adjudicated[S22].

21–22 July 1992

A National Security Council decision on 21 July 1992 produced an operation to place La Catedral under military control and move Escobar and the other prisoners to a military installation in Bogotá[S7]. Justice and prison officials went to the prison in connection with the transfer and were detained inside while instructions changed and the military arrangements developed[S7][S8]. By the following morning Escobar and nine others were gone[S6][S8][S17].

No court has determined how. During the crisis Escobar telephoned journalists claiming to be in a tunnel; President Gaviria said publicly that there was no tunnel[S17]. Days later the army commander was reported as saying there were indications the men had passed through the perimeter fence into the surrounding woods with help from some army personnel[S18]. The Consejo de Estado judgments reconstruct the operation in detail from official testimony and make no finding of a purpose-built escape tunnel[S7][S8].

Disputed and mythologised

Overstated

The new Constitution banned extradition, and Escobar surrendered.

The Assembly did vote on 19 June and he did surrender that day, and reporting at the time said he had waited for it[S13][S14]. But that was a first reading; the Assembly voted again on 28 June and the Constitution took effect on promulgation on 7 July, after he was imprisoned[S1][S4][S20]. A conditional guarantee against extradition for pre-surrender offences had been available under decree since 30 January[S2][S4]. The connection in time is real. The claim that the vote created the protection he had been waiting for is not what the instruments show.

Unproven

The surrender deal promised him five years.

No fixed term appears in the instruments. Decreto 3030 set a one-third reduction for confession and up to a further one-sixth for cooperation and disclosure of property; Decreto 303 added the non-extradition guarantee conditional on the sentence being served[S2][S4]. Contemporary reporting carried estimates rather than a bargain: officials expecting at least eight years, lawyers close to Escobar predicting under three[S13]. His surrender and detention file does not appear in the sources used here.

Unproven

Escobar built La Catedral and owned the land it stood on.

The government account describes an unfinished municipal rehabilitation centre taken on lease[S9], and a reporter who visited before the surrender described builders converting an existing rehabilitation centre[S11]. The mayor denied Escobar owned or financed it[S12]; officials elsewhere said the land had been seized from traffickers, with an army officer speculating about an earlier connection to Escobar[S11]. No deed, land-registry entry, construction contract, tender, invoice or payment record appears in the sources used here — which is not the same as showing that none exists.

Unproven

The jacuzzi, the nightclub, the prostitutes.

These are not one claim. Semana was listing telephones, jacuzzis, carrier pigeons and parties by December 1992, five months after the escape — stated in the magazine's own voice, in an article principally reviewing Fernando Carrillo's book, without describing or quantifying any of them or saying how they were established[S23]. The nightclub and the prostitutes come from retrospective journalism years later, itself attributed to informants rather than observation[S21]. The official record consulted here reaches only "excessive luxuries" as an allegation under disciplinary investigation, and names none of them[S6]. What is directly documented, by reporters who walked through before he arrived, is private bathrooms, a large suite, games space and a football pitch[S11][S12].

Overstated

The guards were Escobar's own men.

Guard selection formally belonged to a committee of the mayor, the Ministry-appointed prison director and the regional Procurador[S9]. What the disciplinary inquiry alleged was excessive familiarity between guards and prisoners, and weapons held by prisoners[S6]. That describes an administration compromised in practice. It is not the same as a private force in state uniform.

Myth

Police and the army were barred from coming within miles of the prison.

Around 150 soldiers were stationed immediately outside the perimeter when reporters visited, with police held farther back[S12]. The government account records an agreement that the public forces would not enter the establishment, requested by Escobar's lawyer, and an express denial that any demilitarised zone existed[S9]. The two forces were not in the same position, and no provision fixing an exclusion radius appears in the sources used here.

Unproven

Galeano and Moncada were killed inside La Catedral.

Three accounts, none of them a homicide finding. Contemporaneous reporting placed the abduction after the two men left the prison, on anonymous law-enforcement sourcing[S16]. The DEA's 1994 assessment places the killings at the prison but discloses no basis[S19]. A 2014 Justice and Peace judgment states that Escobar summoned and killed them while in custody, but does so in unfootnoted background to a case against another defendant, on charges that did not include these deaths[S22]. Two of the three accounts favour the prison. No Colombian homicide investigation, autopsy or forensic record appears in the sources used here.

Unproven

He escaped through a tunnel built for the purpose.

Escobar said so by telephone during the crisis; the President denied it the same day[S17]. The army commander pointed instead to the perimeter fence and possible assistance from soldiers[S18]. The Consejo de Estado judgments, which reconstruct the operation from official testimony, make no such finding[S7][S8].

Gaps in the record

These are limits of the sources used here. The underlying Colombian file is very much larger: one of the judgments records that the evidence before the court included a congressional inquiry and twenty-nine volumes copied from the Fiscalía's investigation into the escape[S7].

  • The founding documents of the prison. The signed lease of 30 May 1991 and the policy memorandum of 29 May are known through a government account reported a year later, not from the documents themselves[S9][S10].
  • Decreto 303. The official copy returns an error; the wording of Article 1 rests on the legislative-history notes to Decreto 3030 and on the Constitutional Court's judgment[S2][S4].
  • The Assembly's own record. The plenary transcripts are catalogued but were not accessible, so both votes are established through contemporaneous reporting and later scholarship rather than the Assembly's gazettes[S13][S14][S20].
  • Escobar's personal file. His surrender, confession and detention record does not appear here, so what he was charged with and the terms on which he was held rest on the general instruments.
  • Ownership and construction. No land or cadastral record, and no construction or financing document, was available.
  • The deaths of Galeano and Moncada. No homicide investigation, autopsy or forensic record was available. The DEA memorandum refers to four members of the two families; the accounts consulted differ on where the killings happened[S16][S19][S22].
  • The escape. The Fiscalía investigation and the congressional inquiry both appear in the judicial record but not in the material used here[S7][S8].
