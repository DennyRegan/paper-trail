RICO, 15 October 1970: What the Statute Actually Did

In brief

On 15 October 1970 President Richard Nixon signed the Organized Crime Control Act of 1970 into law at the Department of Justice's Great Hall, enacting it as Public Law 91-452. Title IX of that Act, headed "Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations," inserted a new Chapter 96 into Title 18 of the United States Code, sections 1961 through 1968[S1][S2][S3].

The House of Representatives had passed the underlying bill, S. 30, on 7 October 1970 by a recorded vote of 341 to 26[S4][S7]. As enacted, Chapter 96 defined racketeering activity, a pattern of racketeering activity and an enterprise; it prohibited four categories of conduct built on those definitions; and it created both a criminal track running to twenty years' imprisonment and forfeiture, and a civil track including a private right of action for treble damages[S1]. Eleven years later, in United States v. Turkette, the Supreme Court held that the enterprise reached by the statute need not be a legitimate business at all[S5].

What the documentary record establishes is the text Congress enacted, the date and place it was signed, and the House's lopsided vote in its favour. What it does not establish, on the sources gathered here, is a formal statutory short title naming the chapter after the acronym by which it is now universally known, the full identity of every official recorded present at the signing, or the Senate's own final vote tally on the bill[S1][S3][S4].

What happened

S. 30 in the House

Nixon's own signing remarks describe roughly eighteen months of congressional work behind the bill[S2]. The House of Representatives' historical account records that, with November elections approaching, the chamber passed an amended version of the measure on 7 October 1970, and identifies Emanuel Celler as chairman of the Judiciary Committee through which it moved[S7]. A White House legislative log compiled a month later fixes the outcome precisely: "By a record vote of 341 yeas to 26 nays, the House passed S. 30, relating to the control of organized crime in the United States," on Wednesday, 7 October 1970[S4].

That document is an internal White House summary rather than the Congressional Record itself, but it and the House's own historical page agree on the date, and between them they supply both the date and the tally for the House side of the bill's passage[S4][S7].

Chapter 96: the definitions

Title IX begins at page 941 of volume 84 of the Statutes at Large, under the heading "Chapter 96.—Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations," running from section 1961, Definitions, to section 1968, Civil investigative demand[S1]. Section 1961 supplied the vocabulary the rest of the chapter depends on: "racketeering activity," "person," "enterprise," "unlawful debt," and "pattern of racketeering activity." The last of these was given a specific test: a pattern "requires at least two acts of racketeering activity, one of which occurred after the effective date of this chapter and the last of which occurred within ten years … after the commission of a prior act of racketeering activity"[S1].

Section 1962: four prohibitions

Section 1962 set out what the chapter actually forbade: investing income derived from a pattern of racketeering activity in an enterprise; acquiring or maintaining an interest in or control of an enterprise through such a pattern; conducting or participating in the conduct of an enterprise's affairs through a pattern of racketeering activity or the collection of an unlawful debt; and conspiring to do any of the foregoing[S1]. It is the third of these — conducting an enterprise's affairs through a pattern of racketeering — that would carry the weight of most later RICO prosecutions, though the statute's own text does not distinguish one prohibition as more important than the others.

Penalties, forfeiture, and a civil track

Section 1963 fixed the criminal consequences: a defendant convicted under section 1962 "shall be fined not more than $25,000 or imprisoned not more than twenty years, or both, and shall forfeit" the interests and property connected to the violation[S1]. Forfeiture on conviction, rather than merely a fine or a sentence, was written into the statute from the start.

Section 1964 ran in the opposite direction from criminal law altogether, giving the chapter a civil life of its own: it authorised injunctive relief and gave a private plaintiff harmed by a violation the right to "recover threefold the damages he sustains and the cost of the suit, including a reasonable attorney's fee"[S1]. Sections 1965 through 1968 dealt with venue and process, the expedition of government civil actions, rules of evidence, and civil investigative demands[S1].

The liberal-construction clause

Outside Chapter 96 itself, but within the same Title IX, section 904(a) of the Act directed that "the provisions of this Title shall be liberally construed to effectuate its remedial purposes"[S1]. It is a short instruction to courts on how to read everything that came before it, and it would be quoted directly by the Supreme Court a decade later when the scope of the statute was tested[S5].

15 October 1970: the signing

Nixon signed S. 30 at the Department of Justice's Great Hall at 10:14 in the morning of 15 October 1970. His remarks framed the new law as supplying "tools" to federal law enforcement, and referred to agencies including the FBI and the Treasury's Secret Service in that connection[S2]. The Nixon Presidential Library's photographic record of the ceremony, catalogued under frame numbers WHPO-4742 to -4744, places Attorney General John N. Mitchell, FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover, Senator Mike Mansfield and a "Senator Fong" among those present, though the library's caption gives no first name for the latter[S3].

Turkette, 1981: the enterprise question

The statute's reach was not settled by its text alone. In United States v. Turkette, decided 17 June 1981, the Supreme Court confronted the argument that Chapter 96 was aimed only at the infiltration of legitimate businesses by racketeers, and that a wholly criminal enterprise fell outside it. The Court rejected that reading. It found support in the ordinary meaning of "enterprise" as the statute defined it, and it invoked section 904(a) directly: "Section 904(a) of RICO, 84 Stat. 947, directs that '[t]he provisions of this Title shall be liberally construed to effectuate its remedial purposes'"[S5]. The Court did not deny that combating the infiltration of legitimate business had been a major concern behind the legislation; it held only that this concern did not exclude enterprises that were criminal through and through[S5]. The Department of Justice's own Criminal RICO Manual, compiled decades later, summarises the chapter in the same terms and confirms its codification at 18 U.S.C. §§ 1961–1968, though as an institutional restatement it stands some way from the 1970 text itself[S6].

Disputed and mythologised

Myth

RICO was drafted to reach only the Mafia's infiltration of legitimate business.

The Supreme Court's account of the Act's own findings, recited in Turkette, describes "organized crime's pervasiveness" in general terms; it does not show the enacted text naming any particular organisation, and the legislative history the Court cites treats infiltration of legitimate business as one major purpose among others, not the statute's outer limit[S5]. The claim that the chapter's coverage stops at legitimate fronts was tested directly in that case and rejected: the Court held that a wholly illegitimate enterprise is covered as well[S5]. What is true is that the concern with legitimate-business infiltration was real and documented. What does not survive is the narrower reading of the statute built on that concern alone.

Overstated

RICO was written as an anti-Mafia law and says so.

The Act is popularly remembered as the law that finally let prosecutors go after organised crime as an institution, and the sources here support the general framing: Nixon's own remarks at the signing describe new tools for federal law enforcement against organised crime, and the Act's statement of findings, as recited by the Supreme Court, addresses organised crime's pervasiveness[S2][S5]. Nothing in the statutory text retrieved here, however, names the Mafia, La Cosa Nostra, or any specific organisation. Chapter 96 is drafted in the general vocabulary of "enterprise," "pattern of racketeering activity" and "unlawful debt," and its reach — as Turkette would later confirm — extends to any enterprise meeting that definition, not to a named target[S1][S5].

Unproven

The 1970 statute formally christened itself the "Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act."

Chapter 96 is headed "Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations" in the Statutes at Large, and every institutional source consulted here refers to it by that name or its acronym[S1][S6][S7]. Whether the enacted text of 1970 itself contained a formal short-title clause — the kind of provision that reads "this chapter may be cited as …" — was not located in the pages of the Statutes at Large used for this article. The chapter heading is not the same thing as a citation clause, and until that specific language is located or ruled out, the point that the Act named itself in so many words should be treated as unconfirmed rather than established.

Unproven

Congress passed the Organized Crime Control Act by overwhelming, near-unanimous consent.

On the House side this is documented precisely: 341 to 26 on 7 October 1970[S4][S7]. What is not established here is the corresponding figure for the Senate. Nixon's remarks refer to roughly eighteen months of congressional work on the bill, which implies the Senate had already acted on S. 30 before the House vote, but no Senate roll call or final tally appears among the sources gathered for this article[S2]. The claim of near-unanimity may well hold across the whole of Congress; on the material assembled here it can only be shown for one chamber.

Gaps in the record

  • The short title. No explicit "this chapter may be cited as" clause was located within the retrieved pages of the Statutes at Large for Title IX. The chapter heading itself is confirmed; a dedicated short-title provision, if one exists in the enacted text, was not found in this session[S1].
  • Senator Fong. The Nixon Presidential Library's caption for the signing photographs names a "Senator Fong" among those present but gives no first name or state, so his full identification could not be confirmed from the source used[S3].
  • The Senate vote. The House roll call of 7 October 1970 is documented to the vote (341–26), but no equivalent Senate roll call or passage date was retrieved for this article, despite S. 30 having plainly passed the Senate before reaching the President's desk[S2][S4].
  • The committee reports. Senate Report No. 91-617 and House Report No. 91-1549, the underlying committee documents on legislative intent, were not retrieved directly for this article. Where those reports are quoted here, the quotation is attributed to the Supreme Court's or the Department of Justice's citation of them, not to an independent reading of the reports themselves[S5][S6].
