Jimmy Hoffa and the Mafia, 1957–1967: What the Government Actually Proved

In brief

Between 1957 and 1967 James R. Hoffa was investigated by a Senate select committee, indicted in five federal districts, sued by his own union's members, and brought to trial six times. Two of those trials ended in acquittal, two in hung juries, and two in conviction. He entered the Federal Penitentiary at Lewisburg on 7 March 1967 to serve an aggregate thirteen years[S19].

Neither conviction required the government to prove a relationship with organised crime. One was for obstruction of justice by attempting to corrupt a jury; the other was for mail fraud, wire fraud and conspiracy in obtaining union pension-fund loans[S16][S18]. Evidence involving men publicly identified with racketeering did reach those courtrooms. What no verdict established was the thing the case against Hoffa was popularly understood to be about.

What happened

Two acquittals

In March 1957 a federal grand jury in Washington indicted Hoffa over an alleged offer of $18,000 and a payment of $3,000 for documents taken from the files of the Senate Select Committee on Improper Activities in the Labor or Management Field. Contemporaneous reporting described the charges as bribery, conspiracy and obstruction of justice, arising from the allegation that Hoffa had paid the committee's investigator, John Cye Cheasty, to spy on the inquiry[S1]. A federal jury acquitted him on 19 July 1957. The dispatch reporting the verdict described the charges tried as bribery and conspiracy, without the obstruction count that appeared in reporting of the indictment[S2]. No judgment or docket for the prosecution was located, so the acquittal is established here through contemporaneous reporting rather than the court record.

Two further indictments were already pending in New York. The first, filed on 14 May 1957, charged Hoffa, Owen Brennan and Bernard Spindel with conspiring to intercept telephone communications at Teamsters headquarters in Detroit, including the calls of people who might be summoned before congressional subcommittees investigating labour racketeering. The second, filed on 25 September, charged Hoffa with five counts of perjury before a federal grand jury[S3]. The wiretap conspiracy went to trial that December and the jury divided eleven to one for conviction[S4]. On retrial, all three men were acquitted on 23 June 1958 after seven hours of deliberation[S5].

The Dioguardi recordings

Hoffa testified publicly before the select committee on four days in August 1957. Ruling on his application to postpone the New York trials because of pre-trial publicity, Judge William B. Herlands asked for the full transcript of the session of 23 August and analysed it[S3]. His account of that day is the most detailed record of the questioning available here.

The material at issue came from wiretaps placed on Hoffa's telephone by New York City police, under court order, between February and June 1953[S3]. Two conversations with John "Johnny Dio" Dioguardi were put into the committee's record.

In the first, from 2 June 1953, Dioguardi told Hoffa he had a couple of "those things", perhaps four, with two available for certain. Asked what they were, Hoffa called the exchange jumbled and said he did not recall. He acknowledged buying Minifon recording machines but did not remember whether Dioguardi had any part in obtaining them[S3].

The second, from 16 June, is more substantial. It refers to Dioguardi's man reaching Detroit and to Hoffa saying they are doing that work; to money for supplies, $500 and thirteen the previous week; and to Dioguardi telling Hoffa these are the best, and that if he needs them anywhere in the country they are his people. Asked what the conversation concerned, Hoffa said he could not remember, and stayed with that answer through several pages of questioning[S3].

At that point Hoffa's counsel told the chairman the recording bore directly on the pending wiretap indictment and would form part of the government's case in New York. Senator McClellan ended the line of questioning[S3]. Herlands held that the committee had been examining Minifons rather than wiretapping, and so had not prejudiced the pending charge. He made no finding about what the calls showed, and the charge they bore on ended in acquittal the following June[S3][S5].

Hoffa returned to the committee in September 1958 for extended questioning about Teamsters transactions and union governance[S6]. A second series of hearings in July 1959 took sworn testimony from Anthony Provenzano and Joseph Glimco, among others[S7].

The monitorship

Rank-and-file Teamsters sued to stop the officers elected at the 1957 convention, Hoffa among them, from taking office, alleging that the convention and the election had been rigged. After twenty-two days of trial the parties settled. The consent order let the officers serve provisionally and created a court-supervised Board of Monitors. Judge F. Dickinson Letts stated that, the case having settled, the court had not been called on to weigh the evidence before it — while also recording his understanding that the evidence tended to prove the plaintiffs' allegation[S8].

On appeal the D.C. Circuit upheld the consent decree while holding the monitors' functions advisory and recommendatory rather than powers of command. The district court had adopted 129 findings proposed by the monitors and had found bad faith; the appellate court said it was unnecessary to pass on the accuracy of nearly all of them[S9].

What the committee concluded

The select committee's final report, published in 1960, set out its conclusions about Hoffa under a formal findings heading. It found that he had misused concentrated union power in a self-serving way; entered business relationships with employers, insurers and banks handling Teamsters money; used union funds for himself and his friends; supported the interests of racketeer friends over those of members; and repressed democratic rights within the union. It further found that he had no intention of moving against those associates, and had not used the powers the union constitution gave him even where convicted officials went to prison while keeping office, salary or union support[S10].

On named figures the report is more specific, and the specificity varies in kind. It stated that testimony had described collusive arrangements involving Hoffa's plan for seven New York paper locals, Dioguardi and Anthony "Tony Ducks" Corallo, and that those locals ended up dominated and controlled by the two men. It found that Hoffa and International Vice-President John T. O'Brien had approved and sanctioned Joseph Glimco as a Teamsters leader, and so condoned his control of Local 777. It described Anthony "Tony Pro" Provenzano as a recognised Hoffa henchman long identified with major criminals in the New York and New Jersey area. It described Paul Dorfman as a major Chicago underworld figure whose family had profited from a close relationship with Hoffa through Teamsters insurance business, summarised testimony placing Dorfman beside Hoffa at two New York wiretapping trials, and recorded their shared involvement in a lodge. For Paul DeLucia, better known as Paul Ricca, it recorded the sale of his Indiana home to Detroit Teamsters locals headed by Hoffa and Brennan[S10].

These range from a documented property transaction to summaries of witness testimony to broad characterisation. All were reached on the committee's own record, by a body applying no criminal standard of proof.

Sun Valley and Test Fleet

A twelve-count Florida indictment charged Hoffa, Henry Lower and Robert McCarthy with mail and wire fraud over an alleged scheme concerning Sun Valley land. On 12 July 1961 the district court dismissed it in full because the grand jury had been selected unlawfully, saying expressly that the ruling did not determine the merits[S11]. A new grand jury returned a sixteen-count indictment on 11 October 1961[S12]. The Fifth Circuit dismissed an attempted appeal from orders quashing subpoenas, on the ground that they were not final decisions[S13]. The re-indictment was never tried: the government dismissed it on 4 June 1963, the same day the Chicago pension-fund indictment was returned[S22].

A federal grand jury in Nashville indicted Hoffa on 18 May 1962 over alleged prohibited payments from Commercial Carriers[S14][S15]. The case tried that autumn was a two-count information under section 302 of the Taft–Hartley Act[S16]; no document establishing when or how the indictment was replaced was located. The trial ran from late October to 23 December 1962 and ended with the jury unable to agree[S16].

The two convictions

What convicted Hoffa was what happened around the Test Fleet jury. A five-count indictment followed, tried at Chattanooga in 1964. He was convicted on two counts, acquitted by the jury on one, granted a judgment of acquittal on another, and had the fifth severed before trial. The Sixth Circuit affirmed, holding the evidence sufficient for a jury to find he had participated in endeavours to reach jurors through intermediaries[S16].

The government's principal witness was Edward Grady Partin, a Teamsters official reporting from inside Hoffa's camp. The Supreme Court's opinion sets out what the record disclosed about him: he faced state and federal charges, federal proceedings against him were postponed, four monthly payments of $300 went to his wife from government funds, and charges were dropped or left unpursued. The Court accepted he had motives to lie beyond those of most informers, and held that cross-examination and jury instruction were the proper safeguards rather than a constitutional bar on his evidence. It affirmed on 12 December 1966[S17].

The second conviction came from a twenty-eight-count indictment filed on 4 June 1963: twenty-seven counts of mail or wire fraud and one of conspiracy, over obtaining Central States pension-fund loans by false representation and diverting the proceeds. Nineteen defendants were tried; thirteen cases reached the jury; seven were convicted. All seven were convicted of conspiracy, and individual defendants on selected substantive counts. The Seventh Circuit affirmed on 4 October 1966, holding the evidence sufficient to support a single conspiracy centred on bailing out Sun Valley, and pointing to Hoffa's concealed interest in it[S18].

7 March 1967

Hoffa entered Lewisburg on 7 March 1967 on an aggregate thirteen-year sentence: eight years for the jury tampering, and five consecutive years for the fraud[S19]. Reporting that week described the five-year term as still working through the courts[S20], and it was. On 22 May 1967, eleven weeks after he began serving it, the Supreme Court vacated the Seventh Circuit's judgment and remanded for inquiry into whether an FBI microphone installed by trespass had tainted the case, expressly declining to reach the substantive challenges[S21]. That litigation ran on for four more years and ended with the conviction standing, certiorari being denied in January 1971[S23].

Disputed and mythologised

Myth

Hoffa went to prison for his Mafia connections.

He went to prison for corrupting a jury and for obtaining pension-fund loans by misrepresentation[S16][S18]. Neither prosecution required the government to prove a relationship with any criminal organisation, and neither verdict established one.

Overstated

The McClellan Committee proved Hoffa's mob ties.

The committee reached detailed conclusions and published them as findings[S10]. A congressional finding and a judicial finding are different instruments. The committee gathered evidence and questioned witnesses under oath, but applied no criminal standard of proof and reached no verdict. Much of what is now repeated as established Hoffa history traces to this document and the testimony behind it. That is a real evidential basis. It is the basis of an accusation rather than of a verdict.

Overstated

The Dioguardi tapes prove Hoffa was hiring Mafia wiremen.

The recordings are genuine — made by New York police under court order in 1953 — and the second is more substantial than the first. It refers to Dioguardi's man reaching Detroit, work being done, $500 for supplies and Dioguardi offering further men anywhere in the country[S3]. That is considerably more than acquaintance. What the calls do not establish is what the work was: Hoffa said he could not remember, Herlands made no finding, and when Hoffa's counsel told the chairman the recording went to the pending wiretap charge, the questioning stopped[S3]. The charge itself ended in acquittal on retrial in June 1958[S5].

Unproven

Hoffa and Provenzano were criminal partners from the outset.

Provenzano gave sworn testimony to the committee in 1959[S7], and the final report called him a recognised Hoffa henchman identified with major criminals[S10]. That is a committee characterisation. No judicial finding in the sources used here establishes an agreement between the two men.

Myth

The courts found the 1957 Teamsters convention was rigged.

The case settled after twenty-two days, and Letts said the court had therefore not weighed the evidence — though he also recorded his understanding that the evidence tended to prove the allegation[S8]. The 129 monitor findings adopted below are sometimes cited as though they were appellate conclusions; the D.C. Circuit said it was unnecessary to pass on the accuracy of nearly all of them[S9]. What the litigation established is that the union was placed under court supervision.

Myth

The Sun Valley fraud was proved.

The first indictment was dismissed because the grand jury had been selected unlawfully, on a ruling that expressly did not reach the merits[S11]. The replacement was dismissed by the government on 4 June 1963 without ever being tried[S22]. Evidence about Sun Valley did feature in the pension-fund conviction, where the Seventh Circuit found a conspiracy centred on bailing it out[S18] — but the Florida land-fraud allegations themselves never reached a verdict.

Overstated

The pension-fund conviction proved Hoffa was funnelling union money to the mob.

The Seventh Circuit upheld a conspiracy to obtain loans by material misrepresentation, with Hoffa's concealed interest in Sun Valley as evidence of it[S18]. Who ultimately received Central States money over the following decade is a separate question, and this judgment does not answer it.

Overstated

Partin's evidence exposed Hoffa's underworld dealings.

Partin's testimony concerned attempts to reach jurors in the Test Fleet trial[S16]. The Supreme Court's opinion also records what he had to gain: pending charges, postponed proceedings, government payments to his wife, and charges dropped or left unpursued. The Court accepted he had motives to lie beyond those of most informers, and held that cross-examination was the answer[S17]. Sustaining a conviction is not the same as endorsing every statement the witness made.

Overstated

By March 1967 the case against Hoffa was closed.

The jury-tampering conviction had been affirmed by the Supreme Court in December 1966[S17]. The fraud conviction had not: affirmed at circuit level in October 1966[S18], still under review when he entered Lewisburg[S20], and vacated and remanded eleven weeks later over an FBI microphone installed by trespass[S21]. It was not thrown out. The litigation continued until certiorari was denied in January 1971, with the conviction intact[S23].

Gaps in the record

These are limits of the documents used here.

  • The Cheasty prosecution. No indictment, docket, transcript or judgment was located. The charges and the acquittal rest on two contemporaneous reports which differ on how many counts were tried[S1][S2].
  • The perjury indictment. How the five counts filed in September 1957 ended is not established by any source used here[S3].
  • The August 1957 hearing volumes. Hoffa testified on four days. Only the transcript of 23 August was obtained and analysed by the court, and the published volumes were not read at page level, so the other three days are not covered here[S3].
  • The Test Fleet instrument. An indictment was returned on 18 May 1962[S14] and the case was tried on a two-count information[S16]. No document recording when or by what order the change was made was located.
  • The testimony behind the committee's characterisations. The final report summarises evidence about Corallo, Provenzano, Dorfman and Ricca[S10]. The underlying hearing testimony was not read at page level.
  • Executive branch files. No FBI or Department of Justice investigative file confined to this period was consulted.
