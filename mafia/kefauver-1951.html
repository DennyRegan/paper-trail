Kefauver, 1951: What the Courts Did with the Referrals

In brief

The Kefauver Committee referred witnesses for prosecution. Eleven of those prosecutions have been traced to federal appellate decisions between 1952 and 1955; others were tried and never appealed[S11].

The contempt cases mostly failed. Carlos Marcello, Joseph Poretto, Joseph Doto and Sam Aiuppa were acquitted on appeal[S16][S17][S18][S14], Martin Accardo was discharged[S19], George Bowers had seven convictions overturned[S15], and seven of Frank Costello's ten were reversed[S12]. William O'Brien and Julius Fink, referred but never appealing, were acquitted at trial[S11].

The perjury cases held. Louis Weber, James J. Moran and — in a separate 1955 case — Doto all had convictions affirmed[S20][S21][S22]. Herman Orman is the one witness whose contempt convictions survived intact, and the reason matters: he never claimed the privilege against self-incrimination[S13].

What happened

From referral to indictment

Senate Resolution 202 authorised the Special Committee to Investigate Organized Crime in Interstate Commerce to examine organised crime's use of interstate commerce, the people involved and the corrupting influences around it, with power to compel attendance, testimony and documents[S1]. It did not make the committee a court.

That distinction became the whole story once witnesses started refusing. Under the procedure set out in United States v. Costello, a refusal to answer a pertinent question could be reported to the Senate, certified by its presiding officer to the United States attorney, and put before a grand jury[S12]. The committee could begin. It could not finish.

In public the distinction blurred. By March 1951 the New York hearings were a mass television event, and the Senate's own retrospective describes Costello as the man who came to personify the gangster on television — his lawyers objecting to the cameras, viewers watching his hands while he answered, refused, and twice walked away from the table without being dismissed[S3]. In April TIME reported a burst of raids and proceedings, twelve Senate contempt citations and five New Orleans indictments, while noting it was an open question how much of the apparent surge would prove real[S26].

The courts answered that question over the following four years.

Costello: what actually survived

Costello was convicted on ten counts[S12]. The Second Circuit reversed seven.

Three concerned his finances — net worth, whether he owed anyone more than $10,000, his total indebtedness. He invoked the Fifth Amendment and the trial court rejected the claim. The appellate court held the privilege had been validly asserted: the committee's own interim report had described him as a leader of a major crime syndicate, and its counsel had signalled what the inquiry meant to pursue, so answers about his money could reasonably expose him to criminal danger. Counts 1, 3 and 4 reversed[S12].

Four more arose after Costello had announced he would not testify further that day. The committee kept putting questions and each refusal became a separate charge. The court rejected that: once a witness has made a total refusal, further questions cannot manufacture additional punishable contempts. Counts 7, 9, 10 and 11 reversed[S12].

Counts 5, 6 and 8 were affirmed[S12]. These concerned his departure from the hearing and his blanket refusals to give any testimony on 15 and 16 March, after the committee declined to adjourn on health grounds. What survived was not a ruling that his financial answers had been lawfully compelled — those convictions were among the reversed. It was a ruling that leaving without leave, and refusing to give any testimony at all, remained punishable whatever the position on particular questions.

One detail in the reasoning is worth holding onto. The court relied on the committee's description of Costello as a syndicate leader, not to endorse it, but because the accusation was what made his fear of prosecution reasonable[S12]. The allegation operated as context for the privilege, not as a finding of fact.

The privilege cases

The same logic ran through the rest.

Doto, known as Joe Adonis, refused to say whether he had ever made a political contribution. The Second Circuit reversed and ordered an acquittal in 1953: admitting the contribution could supply the first element of any number of federal offences, and against the background the committee itself had created, the question was not free of criminal implication[S16]. Because the committee had placed him under suspicion, his grounds for silence were stronger.

The Fifth Circuit reached the same place in the southern cases. Marcello had been convicted on six counts in New Orleans; the government argued that earlier statements to an FBI agent had waived the privilege, an argument the court rejected as to the counts it touched, applying further Fifth Amendment analysis to the rest and entering an acquittal[S17]. Poretto had been convicted on twelve of twenty-six counts after questioning that pressed him to explain the criminal danger he claimed to fear; the court held that a legislative body cannot require a witness to disclose the very details that would show how an answer might incriminate him[S18]. Accardo, subpoenaed to Miami with his income, expenses, tax returns and property records, was held to fall within both decisions and discharged[S8][S19].

The Sixth Circuit reversed Sam Aiuppa's three convictions in December 1952 — questions about whether he knew R. L. O'Donnell, whether he knew Anthony Accardo, and whether his handbook took its 1947 wire service from R. & H. Publishing. Answers to all three could have furnished links in a potential prosecution[S14].

The D.C. Circuit went further with George Bowers, and on different ground. Seven convictions, all overturned, because pertinency was an element of the offence that the prosecution had to prove rather than something a trial judge could presume from the committee's general mandate. The government had established it for none of the seven questions[S15].

Orman, and the limit of the pattern

Herman Orman refused to produce his 1951 book of accounts and refused to name the person from whom he had borrowed $25,000. The Third Circuit affirmed all four convictions in September 1953[S13].

The distinction is not that the court treated him more harshly. It is that Orman had not made a self-incrimination claim at all. He had conditioned compliance on the committee undertaking to keep his information from the press, and the court held that a witness cannot attach terms to a lawful demand. The inquiries were pertinent, so the refusals were criminal[S13].

Even here the court trimmed the result. Counts 1 and 2 were a single substantive contempt and counts 3 and 4 another, so four convictions could stand but only two punishments could lawfully be imposed. The probation terms on counts 2 and 4 were vacated[S13].

The perjury cases

Louis Weber told the committee on 16 March 1951 that he did not know James J. Moran. The Second Circuit affirmed his conviction in 1952: committee counsel Rudolph Halley testified at trial that the inquiry was examining Moran and his associations and held specific unpublished information on New York's policy racket, which made the question material[S20].

Moran was convicted over the other half of the same relationship — his statement that Weber had visited him only three times, perhaps one or two more. Evidence that the visits were far more frequent satisfied the heightened evidentiary standard for perjury, and the frequency of contact between them was material to an inquiry into links between public officials and organised crime. The Second Circuit affirmed in February 1952, rejecting objections to publicity, to a single senator constituting the subcommittee, and to the presence of television cameras[S21].

Weber and Moran each received five years' imprisonment and a $2,000 fine; the Second Circuit described Moran's as the maximum available[S20][S21].

Doto's contempt acquittal was not the end of his exposure either. In 1955 the D.C. Circuit affirmed a separate perjury conviction, arising from different testimony, for his claim to United States citizenship. Citizenship was material because the committee was authorised to identify those involved in the activities it investigated, including whether they were citizens or aliens, and birth registers, passenger manifests, census material and family testimony established that he had been born in Italy and never naturalised[S22].

What the pattern shows

Across these cases, refusals mostly held up and lies did not — but the protection came from the particular question, never from the act of refusing. Bowers won on pertinency, not the privilege at all[S15]. Orman lost because he never invoked it[S13]. Costello lost on refusing to testify at all while winning on the financial questions[S12].

None of it is a finding that the committee lacked lawful power. The courts treated it as a properly constituted legislative inquiry entitled to compel evidence, and enforced constitutional and statutory limits on particular questions rather than on the enterprise[S12][S13][S21].

Nor does any of it settle what the committee said about American organised crime. Costello, Doto, Marcello, Poretto, Accardo, Aiuppa, Bowers and Orman turned on contempt, privilege and pertinency[S12][S13][S14][S15][S16][S17][S18][S19]; Weber, Moran and Doto in 1955 on particular false statements material to the inquiry[S20][S21][S22]. The judicial record resolves specific offences and procedural limits. The committee's picture of a nationally connected criminal structure was never the question submitted for decision.

Disputed and mythologised

Myth

The committee was created on 2 May 1950.

The Senate's own retrospective page gives 2 May, and the filename of the resolution as published on senate.gov repeats it[S1][S3]. The resolution was considered, amended and agreed to on 3 May 1950. The Senate's digitised roll call for the amendment records that date and Vice-President Barkley's tie-breaking vote, and both Moran and Aiuppa identify S.Res. 202 as passed on 3 May[S2][S14][S21].

Overstated

The Kefauver hearings were the first televised congressional hearings.

The older National Archives finding aid supports the claim[S4], but the Senate Historical Office states expressly that this was not the first congressional committee to televise proceedings, tracing that to George Marshall's testimony in November 1947[S3]. Doig's broadcasting history supports the narrower version, describing coverage that developed through earlier Kefauver sessions before New York became a national event[S27]. Kefauver made televised investigation a mass phenomenon. It did not invent it.
