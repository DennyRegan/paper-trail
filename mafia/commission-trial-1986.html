The Commission Trial, 1986: What the Verdicts Established

In brief

In February 1985 federal prosecutors in New York charged senior figures from several Mafia families with conducting the affairs of an enterprise called the Commission of La Cosa Nostra through a pattern of racketeering. Most of the underlying conduct was also charged in the conventional way, as separate counts of extortion, labour bribery and loansharking. What RICO added was the connection: a means of presenting those crimes as the affairs of one continuing enterprise[S1][S4][S5].

After an eleven-week trial in 1986, eight defendants were convicted of RICO conspiracy and substantive RICO offences. The Second Circuit affirmed the judgments, with one exception: Anthony Indelicato's substantive RICO conviction was reversed as time-barred[S1].

What the case established was the defendants' liability for conducting the enterprise's affairs through the racketeering proved against them, together with substantial evidence of coordination between New York families. What it did not establish is a permanent national government of the American Mafia, or the end of it[S1][S12][S13].

What happened

Building an enterprise

The indictment returned on 26 February 1985 charged nine defendants with RICO violations and extortion; superseding indictments added defendants, racketeering acts and counts. At its centre was the allegation that the defendants participated in an enterprise called the Commission of La Cosa Nostra[S4][S5].

RICO makes it an offence to conduct an enterprise's affairs through a pattern of racketeering activity, or to conspire to do so, and does not require each predicate act to be charged separately. Here most were charged separately anyway; only Indelicato's murder predicates had no corresponding non-RICO count[S1]. What the statute supplied was the frame — a way of presenting crimes committed at different times by different people as the business of one organisation[S5][S11].

The government described the Commission as a body of New York family bosses which regulated relations between the families, recognised bosses, settled leadership disputes, approved new members and coordinated joint ventures. During the period charged it was controlled by four of the five, the Bonanno family having been denied its seat[S1]. The published indictment extract makes that structure the enterprise through which the charged racketeering was conducted[S5].

The breadth of the theory was tested before trial. Persico and Langella had already been convicted in a separate Colombo family prosecution and argued that trying them again offended double jeopardy. The Second Circuit disagreed: the enterprise in the earlier case was the Colombo family; the enterprise charged here was the Commission[S3].

The distinction carries the prosecution's whole structure. An individual family could be a criminal enterprise, and a body operating between families could be prosecuted as a separate one — provided the government could prove it existed and tie the defendants' racketeering to it[S3].

The concrete Club

The largest group of allegations concerned New York's concrete industry: a "Club" involving contractors, organised crime interests and the District Council of Cement and Concrete Workers, whose president and business manager was Ralph Scopo. On the evidence summarised by the Second Circuit, participating contractors were protected from labour trouble and followed rules about which companies could bid for which contracts[S1].

James Costigan of X.L.O. Concrete described the mechanism from the inside. His published testimony records Scopo pressing the firm to join, the prospect of labour problems if it refused, and what a stoppage would cost a contractor. X.L.O. joined in 1981, and the extract has Costigan ceasing to participate after FBI agents played him recorded conversations in 1984 — a detail resting on the published extract alone[S8].

Scopo's defence did not deny that the market had been rigged — it denied that this was the offence charged. In closing, his lawyer accepted bid-rigging and price-fixing while arguing they were not the extortion and racketeering in the indictment[S14]. The appellate court subsequently held the evidence sufficient to sustain the convictions[S1].

Tapes, surveillance and witnesses

The case was not built on one insider. During closing arguments the Los Angeles Times reported that prosecutors had played more than 150 wiretap recordings, shown more than 500 surveillance photographs and called 85 witnesses[S15].

FBI agent Joseph Pistone gave evidence on Bonanno family language, hierarchy and rules from his undercover work, including what Benjamin Ruggiero told him about the family's structure and about changes after Carmine Galante was killed[S6].

Angelo Lonardo gave a different kind of evidence, and it needs weighing differently. A former Cleveland underboss testifying for the government, he described families, ranks, rules and what he understood the Commission's membership and functions to be. The published extract also records his own involvement in killings and his admission that he had previously testified falsely. This is the evidence of a cooperating participant seeking consideration, not a neutral account of an institution[S7].

At the opening, prosecutor Michael Chertoff told jurors they would hear the terms La Cosa Nostra and Commission in the defendants' own recorded conversations — the point being that the vocabulary was not invented for the indictment[S14]. His rebuttal summation shows the theory assembled: recordings, surveillance, extortion, killings and loansharking presented as the affairs of one enterprise rather than unrelated episodes. A summation is advocacy, not evidence, but it states the government's case unusually plainly[S9].

Murder as racketeering

The gravest predicates concerned the killings of Carmine Galante, Leonard Coppola and Giuseppe Turano in Brooklyn in July 1979. Indelicato was charged with three murder predicates arising from them, and the jury found he had committed them[S1].

The evidence summarised on appeal included a palm print on the getaway car, eyewitness accounts, expert testimony, and surveillance showing Indelicato reporting shortly afterwards to Gambino underboss Aniello Dellacroce and Bonanno consigliere Stefano Canone. The government went further, contending the killings were part of resolving the Bonanno leadership struggle and connected to the Commission enterprise[S1][S2].

That connection was litigated after conviction. Indelicato argued three killings in a single episode could not be a "pattern of racketeering activity". Sitting en banc, the Second Circuit held that simultaneous acts could form a RICO pattern where their relationship and surrounding circumstances showed the necessary continuity or threat of it[S2].

The appellate record also contains disagreement about how far the theory could stretch. A dissent questioned the evidence behind the loansharking conspiracy convictions of Corallo and Santoro, and warned against turning a role in settling disputes between families into liability for every crime family members committed. The convictions stood, but the objection is in the judicial record, not a later historian's[S1].

Castellano

Paul Castellano was indicted and never tried. The appellate record states that Castellano, identified there as boss of the Gambino family, was murdered before trial; contemporaneous reporting records the killing outside a New York restaurant after the indictments[S1][S14].

Philip Rastelli was also named but severed, being on trial in another federal case; Aniello Dellacroce and Stefano Canone, both originally named, died in 1985. The trial that opened in September 1986 therefore proceeded against eight defendants[S1][S14].

Persico represented himself. At the opening he urged jurors not to be swayed by organisational labels; by closing, the division was unchanged — prosecutors arguing the evidence revealed an enterprise operating through identifiable schemes, Persico arguing that membership and reputation were being used in place of proof[S14][S15].

Verdicts

Salerno, Persico, Langella, Corallo, Santoro, Scopo, Furnari and Indelicato were convicted of RICO conspiracy and substantive RICO. All but Indelicato were also convicted of conspiracy to commit extortion and twelve counts of extortion or attempted extortion. Scopo was convicted as a principal, and the others apart from Indelicato as aiders and abettors, on six labour-bribery violations. Corallo and Santoro were additionally convicted of conspiring to make extortionate extensions of credit[S1].

Every defendant except Indelicato received prison sentences totalling 100 years; Indelicato received forty years on his two RICO convictions. Substantial fines and costs followed[S1].

On appeal the Second Circuit described substantial trial evidence that the Commission had acted as a ruling body over the New York families and affiliated organisations elsewhere, and upheld the convictions against challenges to sufficiency, admissibility, double jeopardy and withheld material[S1]. That finding was made on the standard applicable to such appeals — the evidence read in the government's favour, credibility left to the jury — not as a fresh reconstruction of what the Commission was[S1]. Indelicato's substantive RICO conviction fell on limitation grounds; his RICO conspiracy conviction survived[S1][S2].

Disputed and mythologised

Overstated

The trial proved a permanent Commission governed the American Mafia.

The prosecution alleged a Commission functioning as the ultimate ruling body over the five New York families and affiliated families elsewhere, and the Second Circuit found substantial evidence supporting the enterprise charged[S1][S5]. Jacobs's case study presents the trial as a major demonstration of Mafia structure[S11]. What the verdicts and appellate decisions establish is the enterprise for the purposes of this RICO case, and evidence of inter-family coordination, dispute resolution and joint criminal activity[S1][S2]. They do not establish an unchanging national government operating over every family across all periods. Jacobs's later work puts more weight on individual families as the durable units, and Michael Woodiwiss argues the Commission prosecutions reinforced an excessively centralised model of American organised crime[S12][S13].

Overstated

The Commission Case broke the Mafia.

It removed several senior figures on hundred-year sentences, and was publicly presented at the time as a decisive blow[S1][S15]. But Jacobs attributes the weakening of American Cosa Nostra to sustained prosecution over decades alongside electronic surveillance, RICO, witness protection and increasing cooperation by members — together with the decline of private-sector unions, legalised gambling, competition in illicit markets and the erosion of traditional urban political structures[S12]. His own chronology has major Mafia prosecutions and cooperating witnesses continuing through the 1990s and beyond[S12].

Myth

Castellano's murder was an outcome of the Commission Case.

He was killed in December 1985, after the indictment and before the trial opened, so his death cannot be among the case's results: he faced no verdict and no sentence[S1][S13].

Whether his legal jeopardy contributed to the decision to kill him is a different question, and the contemporaneous evidence is not silent on it. Reporting the day after the killing, the Washington Post quoted Ronald Goldstock, executive director of the New York State Organized Crime Task Force, saying Castellano had become a liability because of the number of prosecutions he faced, and that there was significant concern within organised crime that he might cooperate with investigators[S16].

That is an official's assessment given immediately afterwards, not proof of the killers' reasoning, and nothing here establishes who ordered the murder or why. But the two facts sit together: he was under indictment in this and other prosecutions, and senior law enforcement believed at the time that this had made him vulnerable to his own side[S1][S16].

Gaps in the record

  • The indictment. No complete copy of the operative 25-count superseding indictment was located among the open sources used here. [S5] is an edited extract with marked omissions, so anything turning on language absent from it cannot be checked against the filed pleading. Complete copies may well exist in court files or paid databases.
  • The trial record. No continuous transcript of the eleven-week trial was located among the open sources used here. The Pistone, Lonardo and Costigan testimony and the Chertoff summation used here are selected extracts published in the same volume as Jacobs's case study, and are not independent of it. They cannot show cross-examination, argument or the judge's instructions[S6][S7][S8][S9][S11].
  • The FBI file. The available release is fragmentary and heavily redacted — records of indictments, arrests, proceedings and verdicts, not a complete investigative file or the surveillance material used at trial[S10].
  • The evidence itself. The appellate record and contemporary reporting establish that extensive recordings, photographs and exhibits existed. None was located in the open sources used here, so the appellate opinions are the best account of that evidence available to this article[S1][S14].
  • The larger inference. These sources establish what was alleged, what witnesses said, what the jury convicted on and what the appellate courts upheld. They do not make one prosecution into an institutional history of American Cosa Nostra, and the disagreement between Jacobs and Woodiwiss is itself a reason not to try[S12][S13].
