The Pizza Connection Trial, 1985–1987: What the Verdicts Established

In brief

On 19 February 1985 a federal grand jury in the Southern District of New York returned an indictment, SS 84 Cr. 236, charging thirty-five defendants with running a transnational conspiracy to import morphine base, process it into heroin in Sicily, and distribute it in the United States through Mafia-associated pizza parlours — a conspiracy prosecutors said involved about $1.6 billion worth of heroin/drugs[S4][S5][S12]. The sixteen counts included a narcotics conspiracy under 21 U.S.C. §846, ten separate continuing-criminal-enterprise counts under §848, currency-reporting offences and a RICO conspiracy count[S1]. The joint trial before Judge Pierre N. Leval opened on 30 September 1985, according to the appellate record and contemporaneous reporting; the FBI's own institutional history instead dates the start to 24 October 1985, a discrepancy this article has not resolved[S1][S5][S12].

After seventeen months, the anonymous, sequestered[S11] jury returned its verdict on 2 March 1987: eighteen defendants convicted, one — Vito Badalamenti — acquitted on all charges[S4][S5]. Sentencing followed on 22 June 1987, when Judge Leval imposed forty-five-year prison terms on Gaetano Badalamenti and Salvatore Catalano, together with substantial fines and restitution[S6][S7][S1]. One conviction did not survive: Pietro Alfano, shot and critically wounded during the trial, was absent for eighteen days covering summations, the charge, jury questions and the verdict, and in July 1987 Judge Leval granted him a mistrial[S8][S9]; he was later reported to have pleaded guilty by telephone from his hospital room[S10]. The Second Circuit affirmed the convictions in October 1989, vacating a small number of counts and restitution orders and adjusting Badalamenti's sentence to satisfy the terms of his extradition from Spain[S1].

What the record establishes is a set of convictions returned after an exceptionally long and heavily contested proceeding, on a charge structure — narcotics conspiracy, multiple continuing-criminal-enterprise counts, RICO conspiracy — set out precisely in the indictment and confirmed on appeal. What it does not establish, from the sources examined here, is a single settled account of how many defendants stood on which charges at every stage of the case: the contemporaneous reporting and the appellate record describe overlapping but not identical subsets of the thirty-five originally indicted[S1][S5].

What happened

The indictment

Indictment SS 84 Cr. 236, filed 19 February 1985, charged thirty-five defendants across sixteen counts: conspiracy to distribute narcotics under §846, ten separate continuing-criminal-enterprise counts under §848 — each naming a single defendant — currency-reporting offences, and a RICO conspiracy count[S1]. The government's theory, as summarised in the appellate opinion and in the FBI's own institutional account, was that morphine base moved from Turkey to clandestine laboratories in Sicily, was refined into heroin, and was then distributed in the United States through a network of pizza parlours connected to Mafia figures[S1][S12]. Contemporaneous reporting put the scale of the alleged drug value at up to $1.6 billion — a figure describing what prosecutors alleged, not a quantity any jury was asked to certify[S4][S5].

Seventeen months before Judge Leval

The joint trial opened on 30 September 1985 before Judge Pierre N. Leval in the Southern District of New York[S1][S5]. The FBI's institutional history instead gives 24 October 1985 as the start; the two accounts have not been reconciled here[S12]. The jury was anonymous and sequestered, and by the time the case reached it the panel was working from a fifty-nine-page special verdict form[S11]. The Washington Post reported, at verdict, that the government had called some 250 witnesses and introduced roughly 15,000 exhibits over some 41,000 pages of transcript — reported figures, not a court-certified tally[S4]. During the trial, two of the defendants pleaded guilty to lesser charges after proceedings had begun, according to the Associated Press; the FBI's account separately states that two defendants pleaded guilty during the government's case, leaving nineteen defendants for the jury to decide at verdict[S5][S12].

Violence inside the case

The trial did not proceed undisturbed. Co-defendant Gaetano Mazzara was murdered in December 1986, and co-defendant Pietro Alfano was shot and critically wounded on 11 February 1987[S4][S8]. Both events fed into the defence's repeated arguments for severance on grounds of the case's length and complexity. Judge Leval's June 1987 opinion denying Badalamenti's post-trial motions addressed those severance arguments directly, and records that some potentially prejudicial evidence — including material concerning the earlier murder of Carmine Galante — had been excluded from the trial[S3].

Juror No. 313

On the second day of jury deliberations, Juror No. 313 was excused after her daughter received a threatening telephone call. Judge Leval proceeded under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 23(b) with eleven jurors rather than recalling an alternate, and denied the defence's motions for a mistrial[S2]. The order records the episode as occurring within an anonymous jury that was sequestered during deliberations (and, per contemporaneous reporting, from Feb. 11, 1987)[S2][S11].

The verdict

On 2 March 1987 the jury convicted eighteen defendants and acquitted one, Vito Badalamenti, on all charges[S4][S5]. Judge Leval held the convicted defendants pending a detention hearing and, given the length of the jurors' service, did not have them deliberate separately on forfeiture[S5]. U.S. Attorney Rudolph W. Giuliani and Assistant U.S. Attorney Louis J. Freeh were quoted in contemporaneous reporting reacting to the verdict and characterising the closing argument, quotations that belong to that reporting rather than to a transcript examined here[S4]. The Associated Press separately reported that "five of the seven defendants charged with" the continuing-criminal-enterprise offence were convicted — a figure that does not map cleanly onto the ten CCE counts described in the indictment, each naming a single defendant, and the discrepancy is not resolved by the sources gathered here[S1][S5].

Sentencing

Sentencing began on 22 June 1987. Judge Leval imposed forty-five-year prison terms on both Gaetano Badalamenti and Salvatore Catalano[S6][S7]. Badalamenti was fined $125,000; Catalano was fined $1.15 million and ordered to pay $1 million into a fund for the rehabilitation of drug addicts, an order the judge himself described as unprecedented[S6]. More granular figures for named appellants — including $1,000,000 in restitution ordered against Catalano on the RICO count — appear in the appendix to the Second Circuit's later opinion, which remains the fullest single source for individual sentences among those examined here[S1].

Alfano and the appeal

Pietro Alfano's absence following the shooting ran to eighteen days, covering summations, rebuttal, the court's charge to the jury, jury questions and the delivery of the verdict. Judge Leval found the absence sufficiently prolonged and critical to require a mistrial under Rule 43, and granted one on 17 July 1987[S8][S9]. On 14 August 1987 the Los Angeles Times reported that Alfano, whose "conviction" it described as having been "overturned" the previous month — journalistic shorthand for the mistrial — had pleaded guilty by telephone from his hospital room to racketeering and drug conspiracy. No plea transcript or docket entry was retrieved to confirm the particulars of that report[S10].

The Second Circuit's opinion in United States v. Casamento, decided 11 October 1989, affirmed the convictions of the remaining appellants with narrow exceptions: Castronovo's conviction on count 10 and Trupiano's convictions on counts 1 and 16 were vacated in part, along with several restitution orders[S1]. The court also held that Badalamenti's forty-five-year sentence could stand provided he was released after thirty years, so as to comply with the terms under which Spain had extradited him[S1].

Disputed and mythologised

Overstated

The Pizza Connection trial was the longest criminal jury trial in American history.

The trial genuinely ran seventeen months, an exceptional duration confirmed by the appellate record and by contemporaneous reporting[S1][S4]. But the specific superlative rests on two differently worded claims: the Washington Post called it, at verdict, "one of the longest criminal cases in federal court history", while the FBI's institutional history describes it as "the longest criminal jury trial in U.S. history to this day"[S4][S12]. The FBI's page carries no document date, and "to this day" cannot be checked against later trials from the material examined here. What the record supports is that the trial was exceptionally long; it does not, from these sources, support a dated, settled ranking against every other American criminal trial[S4][S12].

Overstated

The trial proved a $1.6 billion heroin ring.

Contemporaneous reporting recorded prosecutors' description of a conspiracy that had moved as much as $1.6 billion worth of heroin[S4][S5]. That figure describes the scale of what was alleged; the appellate opinion's account of the sixteen counts contains no aggregate dollar figure attributed to the jury itself[S1]. The trial did produce convictions on narcotics-conspiracy, continuing-criminal-enterprise and RICO counts, which is a substantial result on its own terms. The $1.6 billion figure is what was alleged about the enterprise's scale, not a sum the jury was asked to find or certified as proved[S1][S4][S5].

Myth

Pietro Alfano's mistrial meant he escaped the case.

He did receive a mistrial: Judge Leval found his eighteen-day absence, spanning summations, the charge, jury questions and the verdict, sufficiently prolonged and critical to require one under Rule 43[S8][S9]. But that ruling undid his trial result — it did not end the prosecution against him. Five weeks later the Los Angeles Times reported that Alfano had pleaded guilty by telephone from his hospital room to racketeering and drug conspiracy[S10]. That is a contemporaneous news report rather than a filed plea transcript, so its exact terms cannot be verified here — but as reported, it records a guilty plea, not the end of the case against him[S10].

Unproven

Five of seven defendants charged with running the continuing criminal enterprise were convicted.

This is how the Associated Press reported the verdict on the continuing-criminal-enterprise counts[S5]. The indictment, as the Second Circuit later described it, charged that offence in ten separate counts, each naming a single defendant[S1]. The two figures are not straightforwardly reconcilable from the sources examined here. The AP's "seven" may describe the subset of CCE defendants still before the jury at verdict, after severances, guilty pleas, a death and Alfano's mistrial had removed others from the original ten — but that reading has not been confirmed, and the question is left open rather than resolved.

Gaps in the record

  • The trial transcripts. No transcripts of testimony, closing arguments or the court's charge to the jury were retrieved; the account of Freeh's closing and Judge Leval's instructions rests on contemporaneous press reporting and the appellate opinion's summaries, not on the transcript itself[S1][S4].
  • The verdict sheet. Reporting establishes that the jury worked from a fifty-nine-page special verdict form, but the form itself was not retrieved, so count-by-count findings beyond those recorded in the appellate appendix cannot be checked against it[S1][S11].
  • Alfano's plea. The reported telephone guilty plea rests on a single contemporaneous newspaper account; no docket entry or plea transcript was retrieved to confirm its terms[S10].
  • The full sentencing roster. Detailed sentences, fines and restitution are available here for the appellants covered in the Second Circuit's appendix and for the handful of defendants named in press coverage of the June 1987 sentencing sessions; a complete, verified figure for every one of the eighteen convicted defendants was not retrieved[S1][S6][S7].
  • The CCE count discrepancy. Whether the Associated Press's "five of seven" figure and the indictment's ten continuing-criminal-enterprise counts describe the same population of defendants was not resolved from the sources gathered for this article[S1][S5].
