Luciano, 1936: The Conviction and the Recantations

In brief

Charles Luciano was convicted in New York in June 1936 on sixty-two counts of compulsory prostitution and sentenced to thirty to fifty years. The prosecution never claimed he placed women in brothels himself. Its case was that he directed a combination which controlled the bookers, took a share of the earnings and held the trade together with violence and protection money — and the Court of Appeals eventually held the evidence left no doubt he was its directing head[S5].

The conviction ran into trouble almost at once. Three witnesses recanted under oath, an affidavit challenged a fourth, and two appellate judges said they should have been brought into court and cross-examined. One of those judges described the new material as the recantation of three of the four principal witnesses against Luciano[S1][S2].

That dispute was never resolved. New York's highest court struck the new-trial proceedings from the record because it had no jurisdiction to review the discretionary order, then separately affirmed the convictions in 1938 over a dissent[S3][S4][S5].

What happened

The case

The indictment ran to ninety counts under section 2460 of the Penal Law; twenty-eight were withdrawn, leaving sixty-two. They covered three offences: placing women in houses of prostitution, receiving money for placing them, and taking part of their earnings without consideration[S5].

The prosecution's theory tied those individual offences into one organisation. As the Court of Appeals later summarised the evidence, prostitutes worked through bookers who directed them to particular houses and took commissions; housekeepers took shares; money moved upwards; and the women paid a weekly charge for bail, lawyers and protection after arrest. Force was used to extend the combination's reach[S5].

Luciano's alleged place was at the top and out of the daily business. The court described Thomas Pennochio as treasurer, David Betillo in active charge, James Frederico as a manager, Abraham Wahrman and Ralph Liguori as enforcers. Luciano it called the enterprise's principal and leader, directing without conducting its transactions himself[S5].

That mattered legally. The state did not have to prove Luciano sent any particular woman to any particular house; it had to prove he counselled, commanded or procured offences carried out through the combination, which would make him liable as a principal. One strand the Court of Appeals accepted came from a witness named Bendix, who said he approached Luciano for work in 1935 and was offered a job collecting money from houses of prostitution[S5].

The rest depended heavily on women from inside the trade. Florence Brown, Nancy Presser and Mildred Balitzer gave evidence implicating Luciano; contemporary reporting described Brown testifying to a conversation in which he discussed organising houses of prostitution on a larger scale. Luciano took the stand himself and was cross-examined at length by Thomas Dewey[S11][S13].

So the jury was asked to infer control from relationships, conversations and position rather than from anyone seeing Luciano run a brothel. In closing, on Cipollini's later account, Dewey accepted he could not show Luciano had personally placed women and argued he had risen above that level of the business[S13].

The jury convicted on all sixty-two remaining counts. Luciano got the heaviest sentence[S1][S5].

The witnesses

The argument about whether the verdict was sound did not start decades later with the crime writers. It started while the case was still in the New York courts.

Brown, Presser and Balitzer each recanted their evidence against Luciano under oath. Helen Kelly, another prosecution witness, swore an affidavit saying important parts of their trial testimony were false. Muriel Weiss, wife of the witness Bendix, challenged parts of her husband's evidence[S1].

Luciano moved for a new trial on newly discovered evidence — available where the defendant could produce evidence which, had it been before the jury, would probably have changed the verdict[S1].

Justice Philip McCook, who had presided at the trial, rejected the recantations outright. He held that the prosecution had answered the material assertions through its own witnesses, reconstructed how the women had originally come forward, and relied on the fact that Brown, Balitzer and Presser had each reaffirmed their trial evidence under oath in post-trial interviews with him before recanting[S1].

His finding was emphatic: the trial evidence was substantially true and the recantations were false, induced by fear, financial pressure and drug craving. He also rejected the argument that the conviction rested on those witnesses alone, pointing to other testimony and to evidence Luciano had given himself[S1].

What McCook declined to do is the part that has never been settled. Luciano's lawyers asked him to bring the recanting witnesses into court to be examined and cross-examined. The statute gave him that power. He held it did not oblige him to use it, decided he could resolve the question from the affidavits, and refused an oral hearing[S1].

The Appellate Division divides

The Appellate Division affirmed the convictions and McCook's order in July 1937 without a majority opinion. The judges were not agreed on how the recantations had been handled[S2].

Justice Untermyer described the new evidence as the recantation of three of the four principal witnesses against Luciano, together with a statement tending to show the fourth had testified falsely on a material point. In his view McCook should have compelled the witnesses to appear for examination and cross-examination in open court. Justice Cohn agreed with him on that[S2].

Both still voted to affirm the convictions; Untermyer separately thought the sentences excessive[S2].

The disagreement did not establish that the recantations were true. It established that two of the five judges thought the question was too important to decide on competing affidavits and the judge's own prior encounters with the women[S1][S2].

The court that did not decide

What happened next is easy to misread. The Court of Appeals did not review McCook's rejection of the recantations and uphold it.

In People v. Luciano, 275 N.Y. 547, it struck the newly-discovered-evidence proceedings from the record. A later Court of Appeals decision, People v. Jones, explains why: under the rules then governing non-capital cases, the court had no power to review the discretionary denial of such a motion. Review stopped at the Appellate Division[S3][S4].

The recantation issue reached a procedural dead end rather than a resolution. McCook's finding stood and the Appellate Division's affirmance stood, but New York's highest court never decided whether he was right to reject the recantations, or whether Untermyer and Cohn were right that the women should have been heard[S2][S3][S4].

The convictions affirmed

The Court of Appeals considered the convictions themselves separately and affirmed them in April 1938. Chief Judge Crane's majority accepted the organisational theory and rejected the argument that the defendants had effectively been tried for an uncharged conspiracy. Evidence of the combination could prove the individual offences; Luciano need not have known or placed each woman if the offences were committed through an enterprise he directed. On that record, the majority said, the evidence left no doubt he was the directing force[S5].

Judge Rippey dissented. No reasoned dissenting opinion survives in the report — only a one-sentence notation of his grounds: material and prejudicial errors had occurred at trial, and the defendants had been tried for a crime not charged in the indictment[S5].

The Supreme Court denied certiorari in October 1938. That left the convictions standing without ruling on them; the journal records the denial and gives no reasons[S6].

1946

Luciano stayed in prison until 1946, when Thomas Dewey — by then governor, and the man who had prosecuted him — commuted the sentence. The Parole Board released him for deportation. The surviving parole form records the commutation of 3 January 1946 and release on 2 February, on conditions tied to deportation and the possibility of the unserved term reviving[S7].

The commutation became bound up with claims about Luciano's wartime assistance to Naval Intelligence. An FBI memorandum of May 1946 records that Naval Intelligence acknowledged using him as an informant but had given the Bureau no evaluation of the assistance, and carries a handwritten annotation from J. Edgar Hoover attacking the Navy's use of a hoodlum[S9]. William Herlands's 1954 report for the state concluded that Naval Intelligence had originated and run the project and that Luciano and his contacts provided services the Navy considered useful, and reconstructed the process leading to commutation and deportation[S8]. That is a narrower finding than the versions in circulation, which have Luciano foiling sabotage or materially assisting the invasion of Sicily.

None of that touched the verdict. The commutation shortened a sentence on conditions leading to deportation. It was not a pardon and it exonerated nobody[S7][S8].

Disputed and mythologised

Overstated

There was no evidence connecting Luciano to the organisation.

The complaint that nobody saw him place a woman in a brothel is accurate and beside the point: the prosecution never alleged it. Its case was that he directed a combination through which the offences were committed, and the Court of Appeals identified evidence going to that — Bendix on being offered collection work, testimony about Luciano's relations with the operators — holding that a man who procures offences can be liable as a principal[S5]. Whether that evidence was reliable is a separate question from whether it existed, and the two get run together in most retellings[S13].

Unproven

The recantations show Luciano was framed.

Three central witnesses withdrew their evidence under oath, and there is an obvious inference available[S1]. But McCook found the recantations false, made under fear, financial pressure and drug craving, and noted the women had reaffirmed their trial evidence to him before withdrawing it[S1]. What weakens his finding is not its content but how he reached it: he refused to bring the women into court, and two appellate judges said he should have[S1][S2]. The record holds a judicial finding against the recantations and a judicial objection to the way it was made. It does not hold an answer.

Myth

New York's highest court examined the recantations and ruled them false.

It did no such thing. The Court of Appeals struck the newly-discovered-evidence proceedings from the record for want of jurisdiction over a discretionary order in a non-capital case[S3][S4]. Its 1938 affirmance concerned the convictions on the trial record. The recantations have never been tested by New York's highest court, and the sequence of reported decisions can easily be read as though they were[S5].

Myth

The 1946 commutation was an admission the conviction was unsound.

Dewey prosecuting Luciano and then, as governor, freeing him is a striking sequence, and it invites the reading[S7][S8]. But the records treat it as a commutation followed by release for deportation, not a pardon and not a finding of innocence. The conviction remained intact, and Luciano remained subject to the unserved balance of his sentence if he returned[S7].

Gaps in the record

  • The trial itself. No digitised transcript or grand jury minutes appear here. The New York City Municipal Archives holds roughly thirty-five cubic feet of case material — transcripts, grand jury records, witness summaries, correspondence, and a folder covering the 1937 new-trial motion — none of it online, and access to grand jury material may be legally restricted[S10]. Without it, what each witness told the jury and how the defence tested them cannot be independently reconstructed.
  • The affidavits. The recantation affidavits and the prosecution's answers are not available in full. McCook describes them at length and states his conclusions, but the underlying papers cannot be compared with the trial testimony[S1][S10].
  • How the witnesses were held. The women who testified were drawn from the trade being prosecuted, and some were held as material witnesses to secure their evidence. The 1938 New Yorker item relays allegations that witnesses were kept incommunicado and pressured, but every allegation comes through an unnamed lawyer with no documents attached; it establishes that such doubts circulated in 1938, not that the practices occurred[S12]. Nothing in the accessible record documents the conditions under which they were held, and that is a substantial hole in any assessment of the evidence.
  • The contemporaneous accounts. The Atlantic piece is detailed and firmly on Dewey's side, treating the recantations as procured[S11]. Neither it nor the New Yorker item can settle what the inaccessible files would[S11][S12].
