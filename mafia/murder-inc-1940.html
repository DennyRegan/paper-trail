Murder, Inc., 1940: What Abe Reles Could and Could Not Prove

In brief

Abe Reles was not the first man to talk. Harry Rudolph started the Brooklyn investigation as an eyewitness, Anthony Maffetore cooperated next and Abraham Levine followed him — all before Reles offered his evidence in March 1940[S14]. What Reles brought was scale: a participant who could describe killing after killing from inside, and he became the central witness in a series of murder prosecutions[S9].

New York law would not let his word be enough on its own. An accomplice's testimony could not sustain a conviction unless independent evidence tended to connect the defendant to the crime, and the appellate record is largely the story of prosecutors meeting or failing that requirement. Convictions were reversed in Maione over a defective jury charge and secured again on retrial; affirmed by four votes to three in Goldstein; and in Nitzberg the indictment itself was dismissed because the grand jury had never heard evidence beyond accomplices[S1][S2][S3][S15].

Reles did not live to see the last of it. On 12 November 1941 he went out of a sixth-floor window at the Half Moon Hotel while under police guard. A Kings County grand jury that reinvestigated the death ten years later found he had died attempting to escape, and rejected both suicide and murder[S7].

What happened

The witness

Reles had been in the papers before he talked — the New York Times named him on its front page on 3 February 1940, reporting his indictment with two other men. What became public in March was his cooperation. On the 18th the front page carried "Trigger Men Bare 'Contract' Murders for Big Racketeers", though that report concerned confessions from two other men; six days later came "Reles Is Telling Story of Murders Done by His Gang"[S8][S9]. The surviving online copies are photographed pages, establishing headlines and dates rather than the full reports.

He was not a bystander describing what he had heard. The court opinions identify him repeatedly as a participant in the killings he testified about: a confessed principal in the crime in Maione, an accomplice in the Feinstein case whose jury heard about his part in other murders and his previous perjury, a self-confessed accomplice in the killing of Albert Shuman[S1][S2][S3].

That was his value and his weakness in the same fact. Section 399 of the New York Code of Criminal Procedure provided that an accomplice's testimony could not sustain a conviction unless independent evidence tended to connect the defendant with the crime. Evidence showing only that the murder had happened as the accomplice described it was not enough[S1][S2].

Juries still had to decide whether to believe him. Section 399 added a second requirement on top of that: independent evidence tending to connect each defendant to the crime.

Maione: knowing how a murder happened is not enough

Harry Maione and Frank Abbandando were convicted of first-degree murder with Reles as the principal witness. His account matched independent evidence about the condition of the victim's body and the circumstances in which it was found[S1].

The Court of Appeals held that this proved the wrong thing. Establishing the details of the killing did not connect Maione or Abbandando to it. The trial judge had given the jury fifteen items capable of corroborating Reles, some of which established only that the crime had occurred as he said[S1].

The reasoning is worth following, because it is the hinge of the whole subject. If an accomplice can describe a murder accurately because he took part in it, then confirming his description of the body proves he was there — not that the men in the dock were. Treating that as corroboration would empty section 399 of its purpose[S1].

The convictions were reversed by four votes to three and new trials ordered[S1]. That was not the end of the prosecution. Maione and Abbandando were tried again and convicted again, and the Court of Appeals affirmed those second convictions on 8 January 1942[S15]. Both were executed the following month[S14].

What the first appeal struck down was a jury charge, not the case. The court did not hold that the prosecution was impossible — it held that the trial judge had let physical evidence stand as corroboration when it connected nobody to the crime. Told to do it properly, the state did it again and won.

Goldstein: enough independent connection

The court reached the opposite result over the killing of Irving Feinstein, whose body was found burning in a Brooklyn lot in September 1939. Martin Goldstein and Harry Strauss were convicted; Reles described the events and their participation, and the court treated him as an accomplice requiring corroboration[S2].

The opinion is unusually blunt about the material the state was working with. Reles admitted taking part in other murders and to previous perjury. Two further witnesses covering important parts of his account had serious criminal records of their own. The court called testimony from these sources polluted, and examined the judge's directions on corroboration closely[S2].

The requirement was the same as in Maione: independent evidence had to do more than show Reles knew how Feinstein died. Read as a whole, the majority found the jury properly instructed — including a direction that even if they believed Reles they could not convict without independent believable evidence tending to connect the defendant to the murder. The convictions were affirmed, again by four votes to three, with a substantial dissent arguing that the charge repeated the very defect identified in Maione[S2].

Strauss and Goldstein were executed; the Times of 13 June 1941 carried "2 in Murder Ring Are Put to Death"[S11].

Nitzberg: the problem reached back to the indictment

Irving Nitzberg was indicted for the January 1939 murder of Albert Shuman. At his first trial Reles testified that his business was murder in Brooklyn, that Shuman had been marked for death for informing to police, and that he had enlisted Nitzberg, who shot him. Two other members of the same group — themselves accomplices — also testified[S3].

Nitzberg was convicted and sentenced to death in June 1941. The Court of Appeals reversed later that year over an error in the judge's charge. He was tried again and convicted again in March 1942[S3].

By then Reles was dead[S3].

The second appeal went beneath the trial to the grand jury. An indictment had to rest on evidence which, unexplained and uncontradicted, could legally warrant conviction. If the grand jury had heard only accomplice testimony, the corroboration problem existed before any trial jury was empanelled[S3].

The trial and the indictment presented different problems. By the time of trial the prosecution had three non-accomplice witnesses whose evidence satisfied section 399 — though the court regarded them and the benefits they had received with grave suspicion. None had been before the grand jury. Two spoke to admissions Nitzberg allegedly made after he was already indicted; another had not come to prosecutors until shortly before the first trial[S3].

The indictment was dismissed[S3].

The decision matters beyond Nitzberg. Reles could give prosecutors names, narratives and first-hand accounts. What he could not do was remove the state's obligation to connect each accused man to a particular crime by evidence other than his own[S1][S3].

Buchalter: the case that held

The largest prosecution associated with the investigation survived. Louis Buchalter, Emanuel Weiss and Louis Capone were convicted of the first-degree murder of Joseph Rosen, on the theory that Buchalter ordered the killing to stop Rosen giving evidence to Thomas Dewey's racketeering inquiry; Weiss was alleged to be one of the killers, Capone to have helped arrange the murder and the escape[S4].

The scale was enormous — an eleven-week trial, more than 4,000 pages of record and roughly 2,700 more on jury selection. The Court of Appeals affirmed all three convictions by four votes to three[S4].

Reles never gave evidence in it. Prosecutors had prepared him as a witness and he died during the trial; Albert Tannenbaum became the key insider witness instead[S14]. In Buchalter v. New York the Supreme Court, working from a printed record exceeding 12,000 pages, rejected federal due process challenges over jury selection, evidence, instructions, alleged suppression and prosecutorial conduct, and affirmed[S5].

The accessible text of the New York opinion becomes access-controlled part-way through, so the full detail of the evidence cannot be read here[S4].

The Half Moon Hotel

By November 1941 Reles was under police guard at the Half Moon Hotel in Coney Island; the FBI's released file shows federal authorities also intended to use him in the Buchalter harbouring case. A Bureau memorandum of 12 November records the first report from New York — that Reles had committed suicide by jumping — while stating that no other facts were then available[S6]. That was the initial explanation transmitted to Washington, and it was not the one that lasted.

The next day's Times headline was "Abe Reles Killed Trying to Escape", describing an attempted descent from the sixth floor using sheets and noting that police were puzzled by his motive[S12]. The day after, three detectives and two patrolmen were demoted pending departmental proceedings over their guarding of him, and an inquiry was ordered[S13].

Investigations began at once: O'Dwyer ordered an internal police inquiry and a grand jury sat in 1941[S14]. The fullest official document available online, though, is the Kings County grand jury report of December 1951, produced after a renewed investigation. It examined the room, the sheet-and-wire arrangement, the guard posts and the medical evidence[S7].

Its conclusion was explicit. Reles died attempting to escape; the grand jury rejected suicide and rejected foul play[S7].

Two things sit awkwardly beside that finding. It could not say why a protected witness would try to escape. And the same report condemned the handling of the original investigation — guards left unmanaged, evidence disposed of or never examined, delays, and periods of the night that were never accounted for[S7].

Disputed and mythologised

Unproven

Reles was thrown from the window to stop him testifying.

The circumstances invite it: a cooperating killer, still wanted as a witness, dead under police guard. No source here identifies anyone who pushed him, and the 1951 Kings County grand jury rejected foul play as well as suicide, finding he fell attempting to escape[S7].

That finding should not be treated as settling the matter. The same grand jury found the original investigation badly handled — guards unmanaged, evidence lost or never examined, gaps in the night unaccounted for — and it could not explain why he was trying to get out of a guarded room at all[S7]. The FBI's memorandum of the day records only the preliminary report of suicide and says no further facts were available[S6].

So the position is genuinely open: the only completed official inquiry found an escape attempt, that inquiry was working ten years late on a record it criticised severely, and nobody has produced evidence of a killer.

Overstated

Reles's cooperation solved 57 murders.

The figure is William O'Dwyer's, reported on 4 June 1940 as the number of killings his investigation had solved going back to 1930[S10]. It is a prosecutor's accounting, not a judicial count: the same report states that time and death barred prosecution in many of the cases[S10]. The surviving source is an archive abstract rather than the full article, which limits it further.

Overstated

Reles's testimony broke Murder, Inc.

The investigation did not begin with him — Rudolph, Maffetore and Levine came first — and the biggest case of all, the Rosen prosecution of Buchalter, was won without him after he died[S9][S14]. Where he was central, the outcome still turned on other evidence: convictions affirmed in Goldstein by a single vote, reversed and then secured on retrial in Maione, and an indictment dismissed in Nitzberg for want of anything before the grand jury but accomplices[S1][S2][S3][S15].

Reles opened crimes that had been closed to investigators, and several men were convicted and executed on cases he made possible. What he could not do was satisfy section 399 by himself, and the record shows prosecutors succeeding and failing at that in roughly equal measure.

Gaps in the record

  • What Reles actually told them. No complete 1940 confession or interview memoranda appear among these sources. The New York City Municipal Archives holds extensive investigation and witness material, so this is a limit of what is online rather than of what survives[S6].
  • The trials themselves. No complete transcripts were located online, though substantial trial and investigation records survive in the Municipal Archives. The appellate opinions preserve parts of the evidence, but they were written to decide legal questions, not to record trials[S1][S2][S3][S4].
  • The Rosen case. People v. Buchalter becomes access-controlled part-way through. The accessible portion establishes the theory, the convictions and the affirmance; Buchalter v. New York establishes that the federal challenges failed[S4][S5].
  • The 1941 investigations. An internal police inquiry and a grand jury both examined the death in 1941, but their files were not located online. The fullest official account available here was written ten years afterwards, by a body that criticised the earlier handling[S7][S14].
  • The newspaper record. [S8], [S9] and [S11] are photographed pages; [S10], [S12] and [S13] are archive abstracts. They establish headlines, dates and opening material, not the full reports.
  • Why he went out of the window. If the grand jury's finding is accepted, the reason for the escape attempt is unresolved — and so is what Reles would have testified to had he lived[S7].
