Anastasia, 1957: A Murder Nobody Was Ever Tried For

In brief

Albert Anastasia was shot dead in the barbershop of the Park Sheraton Hotel in Manhattan on 25 October 1957 by two men who were never identified in court. Police examined associates, gambling disputes and rivalries inside organised crime. Nobody was prosecuted. No court ever determined who ordered the killing or who fired[S6][S10][S11].

The accounts got firmer as they got further from the event. Joseph Valachi told a Senate subcommittee in 1963 that he believed Vito Genovese had worked with Carlo Gambino and a man the transcript renders as "Joe Bandi" — identified in later literature as Joseph Biondo — and, asked directly whether he had proof, said he did not. Sixteen years later a House committee stated that Genovese engineered the assassination, resting on the work of an organised crime investigator rather than on any homicide case[S2][S3].

The circumstances of the death are well established. What was never established is who decided on it, why, or who fired.

What happened

The killing

On the morning of 25 October 1957 Anastasia was in a barber's chair at the Park Sheraton when two men came in and opened fire. Police never obtained a usable identification of either[S10][S12].

That may not have been because nobody saw them. Hortis, working from the NYPD file, reports polygraph material indicating that three of the barbers were deceptive and that at least one could identify the gunmen but feared for himself and his family[S12].

The first reporting shows how little was solid. The Associated Press described roughly six shots, departure by car and a .38 revolver recovered in a hotel corridor — details drawn from early witness statements and police information, never tested in court[S10]. It also described the gunmen as masked, which Hortis disputes: most of the shop's employees described hats and aviator glasses rather than scarves or masks[S10][S12]. Four days later the same agency reported that police appeared to be getting nowhere, and that Deputy Chief Inspector Edward Byrnes said they had no specific motive. Anthony Coppola, an Anastasia associate, had been questioned after a short-lived vagrancy arrest[S11].

Those two reports are contemporaneous and useful, but they are one reporting stream rather than two — both are Associated Press dispatches[S10][S11].

Theories arrived immediately. Proof did not.

A Mafia killing

By 1958 investigators were saying publicly what kind of killing they thought it was. At Senate hearings that summer, Manhattan District Attorney investigator Martin Pera said the manner of the killing and Anastasia's apparent loss of power had led investigators to treat it as a Mafia killing[S1].

Pera was more careful than most later accounts, though not wholly agnostic. Asked about the gathering at Joseph Barbara's house at Apalachin three weeks after the murder, he said what the men there discussed could only be guessed at — while adding that there must have been some discussion concerning Anastasia's organisation[S1].

That caution is worth holding on to, because Apalachin became firmly attached to the Anastasia story. When federal prosecutors brought a case arising from the attendees' explanations of the meeting, the Second Circuit recorded that the indictment did not allege its purpose, that the government admitted at trial it could offer no evidence of that purpose, and that nothing in the record showed any crime had been committed or planned there. The convictions were reversed[S4].

That does not establish that Apalachin had nothing to do with Anastasia. It establishes something narrower: a federal prosecution arising directly out of the meeting could not show what the meeting was for.

Cuba

Another line ran through Havana. Anastasia had reportedly taken an interest in Cuban gambling, and investigators looked at contacts involving Santo Trafficante and Joseph Silesi, known as Joe Rivers[S6][S8].

An FBI memorandum of January 1958 reports that District Attorney Frank Hogan wanted both men before a grand jury because their evidence might illuminate a gambling motive, and that neither was being sought as an actual killer. The passage is the Bureau summarising a Daily News report rather than anything obtained from Hogan or the grand jury, so it is second-hand at the point where it matters most[S6].

A 1959 Bureau programme shows the same discipline. Agents were instructed to interview John Joseph Coughlin about an account linking Anastasia's ambitions in Cuban gambling, the murder and the Apalachin meeting, in order to obtain material capable of verifying or disproving it[S7]. The document labels the story as something to be tested.

A 1962 report on Trafficante records that he had reportedly been in contact with Anastasia shortly before the killing, in a broader racketeering file assembled from confidential sources and published material. Nothing in it places him in the murder[S8].

The informants

The accounts in government files grew more detailed over time. Their evidential quality did not.

Valachi gave the most influential of them. In 1963 he told the Senate subcommittee he believed Genovese had worked with Gambino and Biondo to arrange the death. Asked whether he had proof, he said he had none[S2].

That exchange is how his evidence should be read. His repeated "believe", and the admission when pressed, indicate he was not describing an order he heard or a plan he took part in but giving the committee his understanding of how the thing had been arranged. That is real evidence — sworn testimony from a significant cooperating witness about what was understood inside the organisation. It is weak evidence of who authorised the murder, and it is not proof[S2].

The Bureau kept receiving retrospective accounts. A New York airtel of February 1969 records a confidential source saying a large meeting at Richie Boiardo's New Jersey estate had discussed the killing and was intended to gauge the reaction to it, with Commission members present[S9].

The account does not hold together. It places the meeting roughly two months before Apalachin — which puts it in mid-September, before Anastasia was killed on 25 October — while also stating that those present knew he had been slain. A second source describing Apalachin as a continuation of the Boiardo meeting was already dead by the time the airtel was written[S9]. This is informant material recorded twelve years after the event, and internally inconsistent on its own chronology.

How intelligence became certainty

By 1979 the language had hardened. The House Select Committee on Assassinations stated that Genovese engineered the assassination and that the Commission approved it afterwards rather than in advance[S3].

The committee's authority should not be mistaken for the quality of what sat underneath it. Its account rested substantially on the work and testimony of Ralph Salerno, formerly of the New York Police Department's organised crime intelligence, rather than on any prosecution that had established Genovese's responsibility[S3]. The committee was not reopening the homicide: it used Anastasia as a precedent while considering whether organised crime leaders needed Commission approval for an assassination[S3].

It was an official congressional finding, arrived at twenty-two years after the event from retrospective intelligence. It may well have been right. What it was not is a judicial finding in a murder case.

Set that beside 1958, when Robert Kennedy asked Genovese in open session what he knew about the killing and Genovese took the Fifth[S1]. A refusal to answer establishes nothing about who ordered a murder.

Evidence did accumulate over those two decades — Valachi's testimony, informant reports in 1963 and 1969, surveillance material, the police files later worked through by historians. What accumulated faster was certainty. Investigative suspicion became informant information, informant information became expert reconstruction, and reconstruction appeared in a congressional report as a statement of what happened, each step stated more firmly than the last[S1][S2][S3][S9].

The gunmen

Here the record is thinner still. No gunman was convicted, and no contemporary identification produced a prosecution[S10][S11].

One persistent story assigns the shooting to members of the Gallo crew in Brooklyn — Hortis documents both a Gallo boast and a later participant account[S12]. C. Alexander Hortis rejects it, having compared the later claims against the surviving NYPD investigative material and FBI intelligence[S12].

He argues instead for Stephen Grammauta as one of the shooters, on a 1963 confidential informant report naming "Steve Grammatula", together with details from the police files about who was around the Park Sheraton and a physical description he considers compatible. The informant account reportedly contained material that had not been published, which Hortis treats as strengthening it[S12].

The distinction holds even here. This is a historian reconstructing a crime from intelligence reports and police files. Grammauta was never convicted of the murder, and the 1963 teletype Hortis relies on was not available as an official scan in the material consulted[S12].

Hortis's work is valuable precisely because it goes further into the police record than the published official documents available here. It was consulted as a full-text copy on a third-party host rather than through the publisher, and its conclusions are a historian's assessment of police and intelligence material, not a court's[S12].

What the record establishes

A short list. Anastasia was shot dead by two men in a Manhattan barbershop on 25 October 1957. Investigators treated it as an organised crime killing. Several motives and participants were examined. No prosecution for the murder appears anywhere in these sources[S1][S10][S11].

The record also shows why a confident version exists. Police intelligence, FBI informants, cooperating witnesses and experienced investigators all settled on an internal Mafia explanation over two decades — though they disagreed about motive, about approval and about the gunmen, and later accounts sometimes inherited earlier intelligence rather than corroborating it independently[S2][S3][S7][S9].

That is not the same as saying the account is wrong. It means the homicide never faced the process best able to test it: disclosure, sworn eyewitnesses and cross-examination in a murder trial.

Disputed and mythologised

Unproven

Genovese ordered the murder.

The House committee stated it in 1979, and Valachi had put Genovese at the centre of his own understanding of the plot[S2][S3]. But the committee's statement traces to investigative expertise and Salerno's testimony rather than to a conviction or judicial finding[S3]. Genovese, asked about the killing under oath in 1958, invoked the Fifth Amendment — which establishes nothing either way[S1]. The proposition rests on later reconstruction and was never adjudicated.

Unproven

Gambino helped arrange it.

The earliest public source for this in the material consulted is Valachi's testimony. He told the subcommittee he believed Genovese had worked with Gambino and "Joe Bandi", and when asked directly said he had no proof[S2]. What survives is a cooperating witness's sworn account of what he believed about the politics behind the killing. It is evidence, and it is a long way short of establishing Gambino's part in it.

Unproven

The Gallo brothers were the gunmen.

Later accounts placed the Gallos and their Brooklyn crew in the barbershop[S12]. Hortis, working from the surviving police file and FBI intelligence, finds substantial reasons to doubt it and argues the material fits Stephen Grammauta better[S12]. But his alternative is itself a reconstruction resting partly on confidential informant information, and no source here establishes either shooter's identity through a prosecution[S10][S11][S12].

Unproven

The motive was Cuban gambling.

Investigators did examine Anastasia's interest in Cuban gambling and the contacts around Trafficante and Silesi[S6][S8]. Hogan wanted both before a grand jury because they might shed light on a gambling motive, while stating explicitly that neither was sought as a killer[S6]. The Bureau's 1959 programme set out to verify or disprove the Cuba account rather than to act on it[S7]. These documents establish an investigative theory. They do not establish the motive.

Overstated

The killing was settled or approved at Apalachin.

Informant accounts later connected discussions of Anastasia's death to high-level meetings around the gathering, and the House committee said Commission approval came afterwards[S3][S9]. Against that: investigators at the time said what Apalachin discussed could only be guessed at[S1]; the government admitted in the resulting prosecution that it could offer no evidence of the meeting's purpose, and the Second Circuit found nothing in the record showing a crime committed or planned there[S4]. The connection is asserted far more firmly than anything establishes.

Gaps in the record

  • No prosecution. Nothing in these sources is an indictment, trial record, verdict or judicial finding about responsibility for the murder. There is congressional questioning but no adversarial cross-examination of a witness or accused person in a homicide proceeding. An official NYPD or prosecutor's case-status record would settle the point more firmly than the contemporary reporting used here[S1][S10][S11].
  • The police file. The complete NYPD homicide file and the District Attorney's case file were not available in digitised form. Hortis reports working from NYPD records at the New York City Municipal Archives, including investigative reports and witness material, none of which could be read independently here[S12].
  • The grand jury. No transcript or disposition of Hogan's inquiry involving Silesi and Trafficante was located. The FBI memorandum establishes his interest in questioning them and nothing about what they said[S6].
  • The FBI material. The four released Vault parts contain allegations, leads and confidential source accounts, and no final Bureau finding naming the killers. That is a statement about the released files, not about everything the Bureau may have held[S5][S7][S8][S9]. The 1963 teletype behind the Grammauta identification was not located as an official scan, so its contents here rest on Hortis's transcription[S12].
  • The open question. These sources cannot determine whether the standard account — Genovese deciding, Gambino assisting, particular men firing — is correct but unproved, partly correct, or a reconstruction in which separate pieces of underworld intelligence were assembled into something more precise than the evidence supports[S2][S3][S9][S12].
