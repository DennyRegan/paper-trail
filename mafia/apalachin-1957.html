Apalachin, 14 November 1957: What the Record Shows

In brief

On 14 November 1957, a New York State Police sergeant acting on local suspicion drove up to a private house in the hamlet of Apalachin and found dozens of luxury cars parked around it. The men inside — senior figures from organised crime networks across the United States — scattered into the surrounding woods and fields. Fifty-eight were identified at a roadblock or picked up nearby[S1].

The raid produced enormous publicity, a federal prosecution, and a substantial shift in how the FBI approached organised crime. What it never produced was proof of what the meeting was for. After 133 examinations of participants by various authorities, investigators could not establish the gathering's purpose in court[S1]. That gap sits underneath almost everything written about Apalachin since, and it is worth keeping in view while reading what follows.

What happened

The background

The gathering came at the end of a violent year in New York. In May 1957, Vito Genovese ordered an attempt on the life of Frank Costello, then head of the family that now carries Genovese's name. Vincent Gigante fired the shot. Costello survived a superficial head wound and stepped back rather than continue the conflict, ceding control to Genovese[S3][S4].

On 25 October 1957, Albert Anastasia was shot dead in a Manhattan barbershop. Congressional investigative records state that Genovese engineered the killing and connect it directly to the power struggle that ended at Apalachin[S4]. Later histories also describe Anastasia's underboss Carlo Gambino as complicit, and Gambino did succeed him — but the claim that the two men jointly orchestrated the murder is not established by the primary record[S4].

Against that background, retrospective law enforcement accounts hold that Genovese called the Apalachin meeting so that other families would acknowledge his control[S5]. That is a credible reconstruction rather than a documented fact, and it should be read as one: the prosecution that followed conceded it could not prove the meeting's purpose[S1].

It is also worth being precise about what Genovese could have been seeking. There was no formal office of capo di tutti capi to be ratified. Luciano had rejected the traditional "boss of bosses" position and established the Commission in its place[S6].

The raid

Sergeant Edgar Croswell of the New York State Police had taken an interest in Joseph Barbara Sr. well before 1957[S2]. Barbara ran a soft drinks bottling business in Endicott, and both the historical accounts and the court record describe a reputation and an evidential trail involving illegal alcohol[S2][S7]. Croswell also knew of a 1956 gathering in the area in which Barbara had taken part, involving Joseph Bonanno, Giovanni Bonventre and others staying locally[S2].

The motel activity in November has two separate strands in the primary sources, and they are not necessarily in conflict. The appellate court records six rooms reserved at a nearby motel for the night before the meeting[S1]. Judge Kaufman's district court opinion separately records Croswell personally observing Barbara's son register for three rooms at the Parkway Motel on 13 November[S2].

At about 12:40 p.m. on 14 November, Croswell and Trooper Vincent Vasisko, accompanied by two agents of the Treasury Department's Alcohol and Tobacco Tax Unit, drove onto the Barbara property. They recorded the registration plates of eight or ten cars near the house and saw more than twenty others further away[S1]. McFall Road was effectively a dead end. Croswell withdrew to an intersection about half a mile down the hill and called for men to set up a checkpoint[S1].

When those inside realised the police were there, the gathering broke up in disorder. Some drove for the road and were stopped at the checkpoint; others were seen walking or running into the woods and pastures and were rounded up in the hours that followed. Those detained were taken to the state police barracks at Vestal for identification and questioning[S1].

Among the men identified were Vito Genovese, Carlo Gambino, Joseph Magliocco, Paul Castellano, Carmine Lombardozzi and Michele Miranda; Santo Trafficante Jr. of Tampa and Frank DeSimone of Los Angeles; and Russell Bufalino, driving the Chrysler Imperial in which Genovese was travelling[S1]. Joseph Bonanno was one of four men taken in the fields, along with John Montana, Antonio Magaddino and Giovanni Bonventre; his case was later severed from the main prosecution[S1].

The immediate aftermath

Fifty-eight men were identified. Only thirty-six were questioned beyond establishing who they were, and twenty-seven gave explanations for their presence. Ten of those twenty-seven said they had come to see Barbara, who had been unwell; the figure later rose to fourteen. The rest gave other accounts entirely[S1]. The New York State Police's own retrospective history puts the number interviewed at sixty-two[S5].

No offence had been committed that anyone could point to. Nothing in the eventual trial record showed that any federal or state law had been broken at the property, or was being planned there[S1]. The men were questioned and released.

The prosecution and the appeal

Public and political pressure pushed the Department of Justice to act. In March 1958 the Attorney General delegated the effort to the prosecutor Milton Wessel, whose group began operating that April[S8]. On 13 May 1959 a federal grand jury indicted twenty-seven of the attendees for conspiring to obstruct justice and to commit perjury, on the theory that they had agreed among themselves to give false accounts of the gathering[S1].

The trial opened before Judge Irving Kaufman in late October 1959[S2]. Guilty verdicts against twenty defendants came in December; sentencing followed in January 1960[S2]. All twenty received prison terms of three to five years, and thirteen were fined $10,000[S1]. Vito Genovese was not among the defendants in this case[S1].

On 28 November 1960 the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit reversed every conviction, in United States v. Bufalino, 285 F.2d 408, remanding with instructions to dismiss the conspiracy count[S1]. Chief Judge Lumbard's opinion identified two failures in the government's case. The first: that men giving similar evasive answers when confronted does not itself establish that they agreed beforehand to lie. The second: that on 14 November 1957 the defendants had no reasonable basis to expect they would later be subpoenaed by federal grand juries, so a conspiracy to obstruct federal proceedings could not stand[S1].

The judgment contains a forceful warning against substituting collective culpability for individual guilt[S1]. It remains a genuine appellate precedent on evidentiary sufficiency in conspiracy prosecutions.

What changed at the FBI

J. Edgar Hoover had publicly resisted the idea of a single national criminal organisation, and the Bureau's systematic intelligence on organised crime before 1957 was inadequate[S8]. But the Bureau was not starting from nothing: the Top Hoodlum Program had been created on 25 August 1953, four years before Apalachin, with all field offices instructed to gather information on major racketeers[S9].

The raid's effect was to intensify what already existed rather than to invent it. Apalachin prompted an expansion of the programme across field offices, and by the end of 1958 Hoover had ordered telephone bugs installed against Mafia figures in Chicago, New York and elsewhere[S8][S9]. The rival Federal Bureau of Narcotics held substantial intelligence on the attendees and supplied dossiers on them to Robert Kennedy and the McClellan Committee[S8].

Apalachin also raised the profile of organised crime in Congress, though the Select Committee on Improper Activities in the Labor or Management Field remained a labour and management inquiry until it expired in 1960[S10]. When Joe Valachi gave his nationally televised testimony from 25 September 1963 — the first major insider to describe the organisation's structure and the term Cosa Nostra publicly — it was before the Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, under the same chairman[S11].

The wider consequence was that organised crime moved up the federal law enforcement and policy agenda. That was a process rather than a single event: the development of an effective federal response took most of the following decade[S8].

Disputed and mythologised

Unproven

Over a hundred mobsters met at Apalachin.

Fifty-eight were identified[S1]. The total present is unknown. Contemporary press figures of over a hundred, and the 120 and 150 that circulate since, are estimates — anything above fifty-eight is an estimate unless a separate evidentiary basis can be shown for it.

Myth

Police were tipped off by an informant.

They were not. The documentary record traces the police intervention to Croswell's observations, motel inquiries and local surveillance, not to an informant-directed federal operation[S2][S5].

Myth

Every man at Apalachin gave the same excuse.

A staple of the retellings, and untrue. Ten of the twenty-seven who gave explanations used the sick-friend account[S1].

Myth

Joseph Bonanno got away.

He was among the four men apprehended in the fields[S1].

Unproven

The bosses met to carve up the rackets nationwide.

This is where most published accounts overreach. Leadership recognition has retrospective law enforcement support[S5], and a released FBI intelligence report records an informant stating that Cuban gambling and narcotics were considered jointly at Apalachin[S12] — but that is an informant report set down afterwards, not minutes or participant testimony. Assertions about a specific policy split over wholesale narcotics, the standardisation of garment district and transport racketeering, or a formal freeze on membership books being agreed that day, go well beyond what the evidence carries. The court record explicitly cautions against this kind of certainty: the government admitted it could not prove what the gathering was about[S1].

Overstated

Apalachin made Hoover admit the Mafia was real.

Overstated. It forced a public and bureaucratic shift, but the Top Hoodlum Program predated it by four years[S9].

Overstated

Apalachin turned the McClellan Committee onto the Mafia.

Apalachin sharpened its interest, but the Select Committee remained a labour and management inquiry[S10].

Unproven

Magaddino's clothes turned up abandoned in a barn, and rival bosses were holed up nearby.

The stories of clothing belonging to Stefano Magaddino found in an abandoned car in a barn, and of John LaRocca and Joseph Zerilli lodging locally, come from local history material[S13]. The appellate record states that no trial evidence established LaRocca's presence in the area[S1]. Neither detail has been traced to police or court records here, which is not the same as showing that none exists. James Lanza's presence at the Hotel Casey in Scranton on 13 November, by contrast, appears in a New York State investigative report reproduced in a federal judgment[S14].

Gaps in the record

  • The total number present is unknown. Only those stopped and identified are firmly enumerated, and men were observed getting away.
  • The purpose of the meeting was never established in court, despite 133 examinations of participants[S1]. Accounts of the agenda written since rest on later intelligence, informant reports and recollection rather than on anything proved at the time.
  • No financial records tying specific transactions to the meeting appear in the sources consulted here. Whether such material exists elsewhere in the archives is an open question rather than a settled negative.
  • How dozens of men were summoned from across the country is not documented in the primary sources used for this article.
