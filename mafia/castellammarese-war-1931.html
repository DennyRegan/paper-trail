The Castellammarese War, 1930–1931: What the Record Establishes

In brief

The Castellammarese War is the name given to the power struggle within New York's Italian-American underworld that ended in 1931 with the deaths of Giuseppe "Joe the Boss" Masseria and Salvatore Maranzano, rival claimants to leadership of the city's Mafia factions[S1][S2][S4].

Masseria was shot dead by unidentified gunmen in a Coney Island speakeasy in April 1931, reported the same month by Time magazine[S2], with a large public funeral following within days[S3]. Maranzano was killed five months later, on 10 September 1931, in his office in the New York Central Building at 230 Park Avenue — a killing summarised in an FBI file drawing on the original police investigation, which records recovery of an address book from the scene[S1]. Institutional histories written decades afterward attribute both killings, and the ruling "Commission" said to have followed, to Charles "Lucky" Luciano[S4].

What the record establishes is that two rival leaders of New York's underworld died within five months of each other in 1931, in specific and separately documented locations. What it does not establish, from the sources examined here, is the exact calendar day Masseria's killing occurred, who ordered them, who carried them out, or that any coordinated wave of further killings followed Maranzano's death[S1][S2][S7][S8].

What happened

Two factions, an uncertain beginning

The FBI's published history of La Cosa Nostra states that by the end of the 1920s two primary factions had emerged in New York, one led by Masseria and one by Maranzano[S4]. The Bureau's separate Mafia Monograph situates the same period as one of transition and increasing organization in Mafia activity heading into the early 1930s[S6].

Neither source fixes when the rivalry that gives the war its name actually began. The institutional material treats the split as already established well before 1930, but no contemporaneous press report from that year was retrieved for this article to confirm a starting point, and none is asserted here[S4].

Coney Island, April 1931

Time's Milestones column of 27 April 1931 recorded that "Giuseppe ('Joe the Boss') Masseria, 44," had been "shot dead by two unknown men in a Coney Island speakeasy"[S2]. This is the earliest contemporaneous notice retrieved for this article. It fixes the death to April 1931, the location to Coney Island, and the method to two unidentified gunmen, but it gives no precise date, no address for the premises, and no names for the assailants[S2].

A specific date — 15 April — is commonly attached to the killing in later accounts. Time's notice does not itself state a day of the month, so that precision is not something this source establishes[S2].

The funeral and the public record

A Daily News photograph, reproduced by Wikimedia Commons and captioned as published 21 April 1931, shows a crowd described as ten thousand people gathered outside 15 West 81st Street on 20 April 1931 for Masseria's funeral, and identifies him publicly as "Giuseppe Masseria, gang chief"[S3]. This confirms that his death had occurred and been publicly reported no later than the third week of April 1931. It adds nothing about the killing itself, and the underlying Daily News page was not independently retrieved for this article — only the Commons reproduction and its modern caption[S3].

230 Park Avenue, September 1931

An FBI file processed under the Freedom of Information Act, compiled in 1961, summarises New York police investigative material concerning "the murder of one Salvatore Maranzano." It records that he was killed in his office in the New York Central Building at 230 Park Avenue on 10 September 1931, and that the 17th Precinct Squad's investigation included recovery of an address and telephone book, extracts of which — names, phone numbers, dates — are reproduced in the file[S1].

This is a government record assembled three decades after the event, drawing on original police files and press clippings rather than being a contemporaneous report itself. Narrative details in it that are not tied to a specifically cited police document should be treated with that caution in mind[S1].

What the FBI's later history adds

The FBI's own published history of La Cosa Nostra states that Maranzano "conspired with Masseria's top soldier, Charles 'Lucky' Luciano, to have Masseria killed," that Maranzano then organised five groups and proclaimed himself "Boss of Bosses," and that he was subsequently murdered by men dressed as police officers, after which Luciano established the ruling Commission[S4].

This is a retrospective institutional synthesis, not a contemporaneous investigative or judicial finding. None of it is corroborated in the primary Maranzano file examined for this article, which describes the location, date and recovered evidence of the murder but not the method by which the killers gained entry[S1][S4].

Conflicting dates in the record

The two killings that end the war are dated differently by different institutional sources consulted here. The Mob Museum's public timeline gives Masseria's death as 13 April 1931[S8] — two days before the 15 April commonly cited elsewhere, and a date not itself confirmed by the contemporaneous Time notice, which supplies no day at all[S2]. The same museum's timeline gives Maranzano's death as 25 September 1931[S7], fifteen days after the date recorded in the FBI's own FOIA file, which draws directly on the original NYPD investigative material[S1].

Where a primary or investigative record and a later museum timeline disagree, the primary record is given more weight in this article. But that resolves the Maranzano date; it does not resolve the Masseria one, where no contemporaneous document retrieved here fixes the day beyond doubt[S1][S2][S7][S8].

Disputed and mythologised

Unproven

Luciano arranged Masseria's murder and then had Maranzano killed to take control himself.

The FBI's institutional history states plainly that Maranzano conspired with Luciano to have Masseria killed, and links Luciano to Maranzano's murder and to the Commission said to have followed[S4][S6]. This is the standard version of events, and it may well be accurate. But it is a retrospective synthesis written decades after the event, not a contemporaneous investigative or judicial finding, and the primary document examined for this article — the FOIA file on Maranzano's own murder — does not name Luciano as the organiser of either killing[S1]. The claim sits where the FBI has placed it: plausible, officially stated, and unproven by the documents retrieved here.

Unproven

Maranzano's killers entered his office disguised as police officers.

This detail appears in the FBI's published history of La Cosa Nostra[S4]. The FBI's own FOIA file on the Maranzano murder, compiled from the original NYPD case material, records the location, the date and the recovery of an address book from the scene, but the extracts used for this article do not describe how the killers gained entry[S1]. The detail is not contradicted by the primary file; it is simply not confirmed by it either.

Unproven

Maranzano's death set off a coordinated purge of his loyalists within days.

A wave of further killings following immediately behind Maranzano's own is a recurring feature of popular accounts of the war's end. No contemporaneous press report or investigative record retrieved for this article documents such a wave, and none of the sources examined here refer to further killings beyond the two principal deaths[S1][S2][S4]. That absence in this source set does not establish that no such killings occurred; it establishes only that this research did not find them recorded.

Overstated

Masseria was killed on 15 April 1931.

A specific calendar date is often attached to Masseria's murder. The contemporaneous notice retrieved for this article, Time's Milestones column of 27 April, confirms the death, his age, and the Coney Island setting, but does not itself state a day of the month[S2]. A museum timeline gives 13 April instead[S8], two days earlier than the figure commonly cited. The funeral, photographed and captioned as taking place on 20 April, is consistent with a death in the first half of the month, but consistency with several possible dates is not the same as confirming one of them[S3]. Something real underlies the claim — Masseria did die in mid-April 1931 — but the precision commonly attached to it is not established by the sources used here.

Gaps in the record

  • The exact date. No contemporaneous day-specific document — a first-day newspaper report or a death certificate — was retrieved for Masseria's killing. Time's notice and the funeral photograph fix it to mid-April 1931 without settling the day, and a museum timeline supplies a third, conflicting date[S2][S3][S8].
  • The venue. The Coney Island premises where Masseria was shot are not named or addressed in the contemporaneous source used here; commonly cited identifications of the restaurant were not corroborated by any source retrieved for this article.
  • The underlying case file. The FBI's Maranzano file is a 1961 compilation summarising NYPD material, not the original 17th Precinct case file itself. The full police file, any surviving grand jury or inquest record, and contemporaneous September 1931 newspaper reports were not retrieved[S1].
  • The war's beginning. FBI institutional material treats the Masseria–Maranzano rivalry as already established by the late 1920s, but no contemporaneous 1930 press report was retrieved to fix when the fighting that gives the war its name actually began[S4].
  • Retaliation and aftermath. Claims of further killings following Maranzano's death, and of Luciano's operational role in either murder, rest on institutional historical synthesis written decades later; no contemporaneous investigative or press record confirming them was retrieved for this article[S4][S6].
