22 June 1941: The First Day

In brief

Germany attacked the Soviet Union in the early hours of 22 June 1941, along a frontier running from the Baltic to the Black Sea. Soviet records and the contemporaneous notes of Franz Halder, chief of the German Army General Staff, agree on the essentials of the opening hours: substantial tactical surprise[S5][S13], heavy Soviet air losses[S6][S12], and serious disruption to command in particular sectors[S7][S12].

Two of the most repeated details of that day are less solid than they appear. The figures for Soviet aircraft destroyed come from Luftwaffe reports made to German headquarters while the fighting was still going on — 800 at half past one in the afternoon, revised to 850 later the same day — rather than from any audited count[S13]. And Stalin did not vanish: the register of his Kremlin office records five men entering at 05:45, and further visitors through the day[S3].

What happened

Before dawn

During the night of 21–22 June the Soviet high command sent a final warning to the western military districts. The directive signed by Defence Commissar Semyon Timoshenko and Chief of the General Staff Georgy Zhukov said a sudden German attack was possible on 22 or 23 June and ordered troops to full combat readiness: firing positions in the fortified regions occupied, aircraft dispersed and camouflaged before dawn, air defences prepared. In the same document, commanders were told not to yield to provocations that might cause serious complications[S2].

The attack

The attack achieved substantial surprise. In the announcement broadcast later that day, Molotov said German forces had crossed the frontier at 4 a.m. at numerous points and bombed cities including Kyiv, Sevastopol and Kaunas[S1]. The Red Army General Staff's first operational summary, as of 10:00, reported artillery fire, air attacks and German ground action across several frontier sectors[S5].

Halder recorded that morning that the armies assigned to the offensive had begun according to plan, apart from Eleventh Army. He judged that tactical surprise had been achieved along the line, and noted reports of Soviet troops caught in their quarters, aircraft still covered on their airfields, and forward units asking their headquarters what they should do[S13]. These are German observations of what German officers believed they were seeing, not a measurement of the state of every Soviet formation — but the two sides' records describe the same morning in compatible terms.

The picture reaching Moscow was fragmented rather than absent. That a General Staff summary existed by mid-morning is itself evidence against a complete blackout at the centre; that its contents were incomplete shows how fast headquarters were trying to assemble a view of an offensive spanning several military districts[S5]. The uncertainty ran in both directions. Halder noted at midday that Soviet international radio traffic, reportedly interrupted earlier, had resumed, and recorded other reports on Soviet headquarters and troop movements whose meaning German officers were still working out[S13].

The air war

At 13:30 Halder recorded that the Luftwaffe reported 800 Soviet aircraft destroyed — 100 by First Air Fleet, 300 by Second, 400 by Fourth. In the same entry he put German losses reported to that point at ten. Later the same day he recorded a revised air force figure of 850[S13].

Those are same-day operational claims passed up to the Army General Staff while the fighting continued. No matching all-front Soviet return for 22 June appears in the documents used here. The air headquarters of the Western Special Military District produced its own summary at 20:00, still working from incomplete reports from subordinate formations[S6]. A report sent to Stalin on 8 July described severe early losses in the North-Western Front's air forces, alongside failures of airfield protection, organisation and communication that let aircraft be destroyed on the ground — but it covers one front over several days[S12].

Command and communications

Disruption was real and in places severe. The North-Western Front's communications summary for the evening of 22 June sets out the state of its links to higher and subordinate headquarters and identifies channels that had failed[S7]. The July report on that front's air forces was blunter still, describing direction of air operations and communications with aviation units as almost absent from the start of hostilities[S12].

Halder recorded at 13:30 that Army Group Centre reported Soviet command organisation in complete confusion. His own assessment at the end of the day was more qualified: some Soviet headquarters had been put out of action and some sectors left without higher control, but after the first shock Soviet forces had turned to fight, and he saw no general attempt to disengage[S13].

The Soviet records show the same mixture. Western Front headquarters was still able to issue a sector-by-sector operational summary at 22:00, setting out the situation of its armies and the movements known to it[S8]. Its information was neither complete nor necessarily accurate — but a headquarters that files a report at ten at night has not ceased to exist.

Moscow

Molotov said the German ambassador, Friedrich-Werner von der Schulenburg, came to him at 05:30, after the attack had begun, to say Germany had decided to make war on the Soviet Union. Molotov's broadcast stated that Soviet forces had been ordered to repel the attack and drive German troops from Soviet territory[S1].

The Kremlin visitor register records Molotov, Lavrentiy Beria, Timoshenko, Lev Mekhlis and Zhukov entering Stalin's office at 05:45, with further visitors through the day[S3]. It establishes presence, times and names. It does not record what was said, who proposed what, or how anyone in the room appeared.

Orders followed. Directive No. 2 told Soviet forces to attack and destroy enemy troops that had crossed the frontier, while instructing ground forces not to cross into German territory pending further orders; aviation was to find German concentrations, strike aircraft and airfields, and hit targets inside Germany to a depth of 100 to 150 kilometres[S4]. A decree on martial law was issued and approved by the Politburo the same day, along with a decree ordering mobilisation[S10][S11].

At 21:15 came Directive No. 3. It acknowledged German penetrations and ordered the North-Western, Western and South-Western Fronts to mount major counter-strokes on 23–24 June, including towards Suwałki and Lublin[S9]. Those orders were issued on the same evening that the North-Western Front was reporting broken communications and the Western Front was filing a provisional account of where its armies might be[S7][S8]. Moscow was ordering major counter-strokes on the basis of a picture that was incomplete and in places wrong.

Disputed and mythologised

Unproven

A precise number of Soviet aircraft was destroyed on 22 June.

Figures for the first day circulate widely and are usually stated flatly. What the documents here contain are claims made during the fighting: the Luftwaffe reported 800 destroyed to Halder at 13:30, broken down by air fleet, and revised that to 850 later the same day[S13]. Those are what German airmen told their own high command, not audited returns — and the two figures differ within a single entry. The Soviet air documents used here are regional and partial: the Western district's evening summary was built from incomplete reporting, and the North-Western Front report of 8 July covers one front across several days[S6][S12]. Higher totals for the day appear in post-war Soviet and German accounts, but those are not documents of 22 June and are not used here.

Myth

Stalin disappeared when he heard.

The register of his Kremlin office records Molotov, Beria, Timoshenko, Mekhlis and Zhukov received at 05:45 on 22 June, and further meetings through the day[S3]. Taking the Soviet government's own time for the attack, that puts him in his office receiving his commanders within two hours of it beginning.

The register settles the question of absence on that day, and nothing else. It cannot show that he was composed or decisive, that any particular order was his, or that he did not suffer shock — it records who came in and when, not what happened in the room. Nor does it speak to the days that followed, which is where much of the wider story about Stalin's withdrawal is set.

Overstated

Soviet command collapsed across the front.

It collapsed in places. The North-Western Front's communications failed in identifiable ways, and its air command barely functioned[S7][S12]. But Western Front headquarters was still reporting at 22:00, Moscow was issuing directives through the day and into the evening, and Halder himself recorded that Soviet troops had begun to fight after the initial shock[S8][S9][S13]. What the documents show is severe and sometimes crippling dislocation in particular formations and sectors — not a command system that stopped working everywhere at once.

Gaps in the record

These are limits of the documents used here, not of the surviving record, which is far larger than one article can carry.

  • Soviet air losses. No contemporaneous all-front Soviet total for 22 June appears in these sources, so the German figure cannot be set against an equivalent Soviet return[S6][S12][S13].
  • When orders arrived. The directives establish what Moscow ordered and when it was signed, not when each army, corps or division received it, or how far the 21 June readiness order had been carried out before the attack began[S2][S4][S9].
  • Geographical coverage. The communications evidence is uneven — detailed for the North-Western and Western Fronts, absent for much of the rest of the frontier[S7][S8][S12].
  • What was said in Stalin's office. The register preserves names and times only, and the fragment used here covers 21–22 June[S3]. It bears on no other date.
  • Later loss figures. Post-war Soviet and German accounts give fuller totals for the day's air losses. None is a document of 22 June, and none is used here.
  • The German side. Halder's journal is his own contemporaneous staff notes, not the official army war diary, and the naval material is an extract compiled from German records rather than the complete operations staff diary[S13][S14]. No translated war diaries for the individual army groups, armies or air fleets are used here.
