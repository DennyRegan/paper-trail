Kiev, 1941: The Encirclement and the Argument About It

In brief

In August 1941 Hitler ended a long dispute inside the German command by ordering Second Army and Heinz Guderian's Second Panzer Group south from Army Group Centre into Ukraine, rather than continuing towards Moscow[S9][S10]. The movement formed the northern arm of an enormous encirclement; Ewald von Kleist's First Panzer Group drove north from the Dnieper, and the two met near Lokhvitsa on 16 September[S10].

The Soviet documents record the trap closing decision by decision. A limited withdrawal was authorised on 9 September. Commanders were discussing the German penetration on the wire on 10 September. Semyon Budyonny asked for the whole front to be pulled back on 11 September and was refused. Permission to abandon Kiev came at twenty to midnight on 17 September — the day after the pincers met. By 21 September Stavka was no longer organising a withdrawal but a breakout[S1][S2][S3][S4][S6][S7].

What the victory cost Germany is still argued over, and the argument is not settled by the record.

What happened

The decision to turn south

By August 1941 Army Group Centre had pushed considerably further east than the German forces to its north and south. The Army High Command wanted to resume the advance on Moscow; Hitler regarded the exposed flanks and the opportunities in Ukraine — its agriculture, its industry, and the security of the southern front — as the more pressing problem. Stahel describes a strategic deadlock running from the third week of July until 23 August, broken when Hitler overruled the army leadership[S9].

Ziemke and Bauer date the decisive order to 21 August: Second Army and Second Panzer Group to go south, other mobile and air elements diverted north. Guderian met Hitler on 23 August and failed to have it reversed. The southward movement began on 25 August[S10].

The danger to the Soviet Southwestern Front had been visible before the Germans moved. Ziemke and Bauer record that Soviet headquarters had recognised the possibility of Guderian turning south by 19 August. Stavka nevertheless restated the requirement to hold Kiev and the lower Dnieper[S10].

9 September: a partial withdrawal

By early September Stavka was willing to give ground, but not the salient. Directive No. 001781, issued at 05:45 on 9 September, authorised the 5th Army and the right flank of the 37th Army to withdraw behind the Desna. The Kiev fortified region was still to be held[S1].

The distinction is the whole story in miniature. The northern flank was adjusted; the position that mattered was not.

10 September: the wire

The direct-wire records of 10 September survive as certified copies checked against the telegraph tape, and they show commanders handling the penetration in real time. Southwestern Front commander Mikhail Kirponos told Chief of the General Staff Boris Shaposhnikov that German forces were approaching the rear of the 21st Army, and asked for cavalry towards Putivl[S2]. Shaposhnikov spoke separately to Budyonny, commander-in-chief of the Southwestern Direction, about moving the Second Cavalry Corps to the same place and how it should be used[S3].

These are not the records of a command that had failed to notice. They are the records of one meeting a deep armoured penetration with redeployments while the order to hold Kiev remained in force[S1][S2][S3].

Meanwhile Guderian's spearheads passed Konotop, and on the same day Kleist began attacking north out of the Kremenchug bridgehead[S10]. The two armoured forces were now converging behind the main body of the Southwestern Front.

11 September: the request and the refusal

Budyonny put the question to Stavka directly. His memorandum of 11 September stated that withdrawal of the Southwestern Front from the Kiev area had become urgently necessary because of the threat of encirclement. It also recorded what Shaposhnikov had told him: Stavka took the opposite view, and expected operations by the 26th Army to improve the position[S4].

Stalin was on the wire to Kirponos the same day, discussing the German penetrations at Romny and Nezhin and the measures the front proposed as its eastern communications came under threat[S5]. Ziemke and Bauer give the sequence plainly: Budyonny asked to withdraw, Stalin refused, and Budyonny was replaced by Semyon Timoshenko[S10]. No documented reason for the dismissal appears in these sources — only the order of events.

By this point the geometry had turned against the Soviet armies. Once Kleist was moving north, Ziemke and Bauer judge that much of the force inside the salient had further to travel to escape than the German spearheads had to travel to close the ring[S10].

16–17 September: too late

The pincers met near Lokhvitsa, south of Romny, on 16 September[S10].

Stavka's Directive No. 002087, permitting abandonment of the Kiev fortified region and the city, was issued at 23:40 on 17 September, with the 37th Army to move to and defend the eastern bank of the Dnieper[S6]. It came a day after the encirclement had closed. An orderly general withdrawal was no longer available; what remained was escape[S6][S10].

20–21 September: breaking out

Within days the instructions had changed in kind. On 20 September the General Staff issued directions to the Southwestern Front and the 5th, 21st and 26th Armies on which way to break out of encirclement[S11]. On 21 September Instructions No. 002227 followed, telling the commands of the Southwestern Front, the 21st Army and the 5th Army how to get through the German ring — in separate groupings attacking through different sectors, rather than attempting to move as formations[S7].

These are orders for a front that had been broken into pieces. Army headquarters and organised groups still existed and were still receiving instructions; what no longer existed was the ability to manoeuvre as a front.

Ziemke and Bauer record that the Germans spent roughly another week reducing the pocket[S10].

What it cost

On 6 September, before the pocket closed, Hitler had already issued Directive No. 35 providing for the main effort to return to Army Group Centre, and by late September its panzer formations were being recalled for the advance that became Operation Typhoon[S10].

That chronology is the basis of the enduring argument: that the weeks spent in Ukraine cost Germany the time it needed to take Moscow before the autumn mud and the winter. Stahel rejects the simple form of it. He argues that by late August Barbarossa had already failed in its central purpose — destroying Soviet resistance in a short campaign — and that German mobile formations were suffering mechanical and personnel attrition that an immediate drive on Moscow would not have cured. By the end of September the motorised divisions were badly worn, supply was inadequate, and losses were mounting. On his reading, Kiev did not create Germany's strategic crisis, and winning it did not resolve one[S9].

Disputed and mythologised

Overstated

Turning south cost Germany Moscow.

The diversion is real, the delay is real, and the dispute inside the German command was real: the army wanted Moscow, Hitler sent the panzers to Ukraine, and the main effort did not return to the centre until Kiev was nearly finished[S9][S10]. What does not follow is the monocausal version. Stahel's objection goes deeper than the conclusion: he challenges the premise. On his argument the original Barbarossa concept — destroying Soviet resistance in one short campaign — was already beyond German reach by late August, before Kiev was decided, and the attrition of the mobile formations would have applied to a Moscow offensive too[S9]. The record establishes the delay and the disagreement. It cannot establish what an August attack on Moscow would have achieved, and no source here claims to.

Overstated

Stalin simply refused to let anyone withdraw.

He refused the withdrawal that mattered, and Budyonny was replaced shortly after asking for it[S4][S10]. But Stavka had authorised the 5th Army and part of the 37th back to the Desna on 9 September[S1], and it released Kiev itself on 17 September[S6]. The picture in the documents is not blanket refusal; it is a series of partial concessions granted too slowly against a pincer movement that was faster than the decisions.

Unproven

A single figure for Soviet losses at Kiev.

Three numbers circulate and none is a contemporaneous count. Ziemke and Bauer record the German claim of 665,000 prisoners taken in the encirclement[S10]. Krivosheev's 1993 statistical study gives 700,544 Soviet personnel losses for the Kiev Strategic Defensive Operation of 7 July to 26 September — 616,304 irrecoverable, 84,240 sanitary — which is a wider operation over a longer period and counts categories other than prisoners[S8]. Ziemke and Bauer also reproduce a Soviet accounting giving the Southwestern Front 677,085 men at the start of the battle and 150,541 at the end, which measures something different again[S10]. They are not competing versions of one number; they are three different measurements.

Gaps in the record

  • The German side. No readable German primary document covering the August decision is used here. Halder's diary for that period, the Führer directives and the Army Group Centre war diaries exist online but were not accessible in usable form, so the German decision rests on Stahel and on Ziemke and Bauer[S9][S10].
  • What the orders achieved. The Soviet directives and wire records establish what was ordered and discussed at particular moments. They do not establish that orders reached subordinate headquarters in time, or that units were in a condition to carry them out[S1][S2][S3][S4][S5][S6][S7].
  • Losses. No contemporaneous Soviet return for the pocket appears here, and Krivosheev's figure covers the whole strategic operation from 7 July, not the September encirclement alone[S8][S10].
  • The breakout orders. The Russian scans establish the existence, dates, recipients and purpose of the 20 and 21 September instructions. The detailed tactical wording was not fully recoverable from the pages used here[S7][S11].
  • The counterfactual. The record shows the diversion, the time it took, and the later return of the main effort to the centre. It cannot show what would have happened otherwise[S9][S10].
