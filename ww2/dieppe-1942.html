The Dieppe Raid, 19 August 1942: What the Despatches Established

In brief

On 19 August 1942 an Allied force landed on the beaches of Dieppe and along the coast to either side of it, under the codename Operation Jubilee. The force was overwhelmingly Canadian, supported by British Commandos, a small contingent of United States Rangers, and Royal Navy and Allied air support. Command was divided three ways: Captain J. Hughes-Hallett, Royal Navy, as Naval Force Commander; Major-General J. H. Roberts of 2nd Canadian Infantry Division as Military Force Commander; and Air Vice-Marshal Trafford Leigh-Mallory as Air Force Commander[S1][S3][S4].

The flank assaults against the coastal batteries at Berneval and Varengeville produced sharply different results, and the frontal assault on the town itself was costly. Of 4,963 Canadians who embarked, 2,210 returned, many of them wounded; Canadian casualties totalled 3,367, including 1,946 taken prisoner and 916 killed. The Royal Navy recorded 75 killed or died of wounds and 269 missing or taken prisoner; the Royal Air Force recorded 62 fatalities[S4].

What the record establishes is the plan as issued, the disposition and rough scale of the force, the fate of the two flank attacks, the casualty totals compiled institutionally after the war, and the official assessment the Naval Force Commander submitted a fortnight after the raid. What it does not establish is a single, reconciled account of the air battle fought over the beaches that day, a verified account of why the operation was mounted beyond the objectives stated in its own planning papers, or a demonstrated causal line from Dieppe's failures to the success of later landings[S1][S3][S5].

What happened

From Rutter to Jubilee

Dieppe was examined as a target by Combined Operations Headquarters from early April 1942, and an Outline Plan was completed by 25 April. That plan set out the objectives the raid was to serve: destroying the coastal defences, the aerodrome at St Aubin, radar stations, and power, dock, rail and petrol facilities; removing invasion barges from the harbour; taking "secret documents" from the divisional headquarters at Arques; and capturing prisoners[S8].

A first attempt, codenamed Rutter, was cancelled on 7 July 1942 after complications, and the operation was remounted with modifications as Jubilee. Captain Hughes-Hallett was appointed Naval Force Commander on 17 July[S3]. Admiral Lord Louis Mountbatten, Chief of Combined Operations, held the organisational and strategic role behind the planning, and its later staff account was drawn largely from Combined Operations Headquarters' own "Combined Report" on the raid[S3][S7].

The plan on paper

The scheme called for landings at five points along roughly sixteen kilometres of coast, with flank attacks going in first and the assault on the town itself following[S4]. The flank landings were timed for around nautical twilight, approximately 04:50, with the main frontal assault following about thirty minutes later[S3].

Fifty-eight Churchill tanks were embarked to support the main landing; of the thirty allocated to the first wave, twenty-seven got ashore[S3]. Roughly fifty United States Rangers took part alongside the Commandos[S9].

Berneval and Varengeville

The two flank attacks diverged sharply. In the east, No. 3 Commando's approach to Berneval was disrupted by encounters at sea during the night, and only a small party of the Commando reached and engaged the battery there. Hughes-Hallett's despatch singles out Major Peter Young for having, with that small party, diverted the battery's fire during the action[S1][S3].

In the west, No. 4 Commando's attack on the battery at Varengeville succeeded, and the Commando withdrew with relatively light losses — a result reported publicly within days as one of the raid's few unambiguous successes[S3][S5].

The German assessment

A German intelligence assessment survives from close to the event. The intelligence section of LXXXI Army Corps produced a report dated 22 August 1942, three days after the raid, drawing on captured British orders and documents. It identified the Allied order of battle — 2nd Canadian Infantry Division together with No. 3 and No. 4 Commandos — named Major-General Roberts as the ground commander and Mountbatten "presumably" as the overall commander, and set out what the German staff understood the Allied mission to have been: to occupy Dieppe and its surroundings, disable installations, take prisoners, and seize "the largest possible number" of documents, weapons and equipment before a planned re-embarkation by 15:30[S2].

This is a German staff's reading of captured British planning material soon after the event, not a neutral summary of Allied intentions, and its qualified attributions — "presumably" for Mountbatten's role — mark its own uncertainty[S2].

Casualties and the return

The institutional Canadian figures, compiled after the war, are the fullest cited here. Of 4,963 Canadians who embarked, 2,210 returned, many of them wounded. Total Canadian casualties reached 3,367, of whom 1,946 were taken prisoner and 916 were killed. The Royal Navy's losses were recorded as 75 killed or died of wounds and 269 missing or taken prisoner; the Royal Air Force recorded 62 fatalities[S4].

These are the figures given by Veterans Affairs Canada as an institutional summary; they are not identical in every respect to the force totals given in the British official naval history, which puts the embarked Canadian Army contingent at 4,961 rather than 4,963, alongside 1,507 Commandos and a small number of American Rangers, supported by sixty-seven allocated air squadrons. Veterans Affairs Canada's own account of the wider force gives nearly 5,000 Canadians among 6,100 troops overall, about 1,000 British Commandos, about fifty U.S. Rangers, and seventy-four Allied squadrons including eight from the Royal Canadian Air Force[S3][S4]. Both are official or institutional sources; the discrepancy in totals and squadron counts is not resolved between them here.

How it was reported at the time

Two days after the raid, wire-service reporting carried in New Zealand's Greymouth Evening Star reproduced Combined Operations communiqués and journalists' accounts framing the operation officially as a "reconnaissance in force," publicising No. 4 Commando's success at Varengeville and acknowledging the failure at Berneval. The same reporting carried preliminary air-battle claims: 91 German aircraft shot down against a reported 98 RAF aircraft lost, with 30 of those pilots reported safe[S5].

These were the figures released within forty-eight hours of the action, drawn from communiqués issued while the operation was still being assessed. They are documented here as the contemporaneous public account of the air battle, not as a settled tally; the UK official naval history later gave 106 Allied aircraft lost and 48 German, but totals vary across sources and a full reconciliation was not attempted here[S5][S3].

The despatch and its lessons

Captain Hughes-Hallett submitted his official naval despatch on the operation on 30 August 1942; it was published as a supplement to The London Gazette in August 1947. It commended Major Young's action at Berneval, praised the fighter cover provided by No. 11 Group as "magnificent," and drew what it called the "principal lesson" for future large-scale re-entries into occupied France — chiefly, the need for greater weight of force and adequate reserves[S1].

The British official naval history takes a more critical view of the same evidence, identifying serious defects in the plan: reliance on surprise that was not achieved, inadequate naval and air bombardment in support of the landings, and a scheme too complex and inflexible to adapt once ashore. It records that the raid's lessons were subsequently discussed by the War Cabinet in connection with later landings[S3]. Both accounts are official; one is the responsible commander's own report, the other a later synthesis, and their emphases differ accordingly.

Disputed and mythologised

Overstated

Dieppe's failures directly taught the lessons that made D-Day a success.

Something real sits under this claim. The Naval Force Commander's own despatch drew explicit "lessons" about the weight of force and reserves needed "when the time comes to re-enter France on a large scale," and the British official naval history records that the raid's lessons were discussed by the War Cabinet in relation to subsequent landings[S1][S3]. But both of those are official assessments made by the institutions responsible for the raid and for later planning — they are not a documented, traceable chain showing which specific decisions in 1944 were changed because of Dieppe rather than for other reasons. The sources here support that lessons were drawn and discussed; they do not support a demonstrated causal link between Dieppe's specific failures and D-Day's specific successes.

Unproven

The raid's real purpose was to seize German cipher equipment.

The Outline Plan's stated objectives include removing "secret documents" from the German divisional headquarters at Arques and capturing prisoners and equipment generally, and the German intelligence assessment likewise recorded an Allied intention to seize "the largest possible number" of documents, weapons and equipment[S2][S8]. Neither of these establishes a specific intelligence-gathering purpose beyond what the planning papers state in general terms. No primary document setting out a special "pinch" mission for cipher material was retrieved for this article, and the claim cannot be confirmed or ruled out on the sources used here. It remains open.

Unproven

Ninety-one German aircraft were shot down for the loss of ninety-eight RAF machines.

These figures were published within two days of the raid as part of official communiqués reproduced in contemporaneous press reporting[S5]. They were preliminary claims. The UK official naval history later reported 106 Allied and 48 German aircraft lost, but figures differ in other accounts and we have not attempted a full reconciliation here[S3]. The figures may or may not correspond to the eventual accounting; on the present evidence neither can be said with confidence, and the number that circulates as "the Dieppe air battle" total should be treated as an early wartime claim rather than a settled figure.

Overstated

The exact size of the raiding force is precisely known.

The broad scale is clear enough: several thousand men, overwhelmingly Canadian, supported by British Commandos and a small American contingent. But the precise tallies vary between official and institutional sources compiled after the fact. The British official naval history gives 4,961 Canadian Army personnel, 1,507 Commandos and sixty-seven allocated air squadrons; Veterans Affairs Canada gives nearly 5,000 Canadians of a 6,100-strong total, about 1,000 Commandos and seventy-four squadrons[S3][S4]. Neither source explains the basis of its count in enough detail to reconcile the difference — whether it reflects troops allocated against troops embarked, or squadrons assigned against squadrons actually flown. The scale of the force is established; its arithmetic to the last man and aircraft is not.

Gaps in the record

  • The air battle. Contemporaneous communiqués gave preliminary claims for aircraft lost on each side within forty-eight hours of the raid. No later reconciled RAF operational summary or comparable German accounting was retrieved for this article, so a settled total for the day's air fighting cannot be given here[S5].
  • German casualties. The sources used here establish German intelligence's own reading of the raid three days afterwards, but no German casualty return or Wehrmacht communiqué for the action was retrieved, so losses on the German side are not covered by this research[S2].
  • The Combined Report. Both the Canadian staff narrative used here and other secondary literature on the raid draw on Combined Operations Headquarters' full "Combined Report" (C.B. 04244 / BR 1887). That report itself was not retrieved or opened for this article, so claims resting on it are one step removed from the underlying record[S7].
  • The "pinch" question. Whether the raid was intended, beyond its stated general objectives, as a mission to obtain specific cipher material was not established one way or the other by any source opened for this article, and is treated as unresolved rather than confirmed or dismissed[S2][S8].
  • Force totals. The discrepancy between the British official naval history's and Veterans Affairs Canada's figures for troops embarked and squadrons allocated could not be reconciled from the sources used here, and no document explaining the difference in counting method was located[S3][S4].
