Before Moscow: Why Operation Typhoon Failed

In brief

Operation Typhoon opened at the end of September 1941 — Second Panzer Group on 30 September, the rest of Army Group Centre on 2 October — with a breakthrough that encircled much of three Soviet fronts[S11]. It did not take Moscow. By late October Army Group Centre had almost stopped; after the ground froze it advanced again in November and reached points about twelve miles from the city, with steadily diminishing strength and no decisive breakthrough[S11].

The turn can be followed closely across nine days. Zhukov put a counter-offensive plan to Stalin on 30 November. The next day Fedor von Bock warned that the condition of his troops was extremely strained and that continuing risked a breakdown. Stavka fixed 5–6 December for the attack. By 8 December Bock was ordering Third Panzer Group to avoid encirclement[S4][S5][S6][S10].

What happened

Typhoon

Army Group Centre's plan of 26 September set out another great encirclement: the main weight in the centre, armoured forces to break through and envelop the Soviet armies west of Vyazma before continuing east[S1].

Second Panzer Group attacked on 30 September, the remainder of the army group on 2 October. Within a week German armour had enveloped six Soviet armies around Vyazma and forced much of the Bryansk Front into separate pockets. German headquarters treated it as another enormous success, though fighting around the pockets continued and some Soviet troops got out[S11].

Zhukov took command of the combined Western and Reserve Fronts on 10 October. The same day Stavka ordered the defence organised along the Mozhaisk line, using whatever could be assembled between the German advance and the capital[S2][S11].

Then the advance slowed. Rain, snow and the traffic of an advancing army turned the roads to mud; spearheads stretched out along routes that could barely carry them, and Soviet resistance continued. By the end of October Army Group Centre was practically stationary on a line from Kalinin to the Oka, its centre about thirty-five miles from Moscow[S11].

The pause was not an abandonment. Army Group Centre's November plan made improved supply and the condition of the ground prerequisites for resuming operations[S3]. Hard ground, in the German conception, was not the obstacle. It was among the conditions expected to make movement possible again.

November

The attack resumed in mid-November, Third and Fourth Panzer Groups pressing round Moscow from the north while Second Panzer Army worked around Tula in the south[S11].

Soviet reinforcement was substantial. Ziemke and Bauer record Western Front receiving eleven rifle divisions, sixteen tank brigades and forty artillery regiments in late October, then about 100,000 troops, 300 tanks and 2,000 guns in the first half of November, and a further nine rifle divisions, two cavalry divisions, eight rifle brigades, six tank brigades and ten independent tank battalions in late November[S11].

The German advance still produced positions that looked lethal for Moscow. On 27 November the 7th Panzer Division reached the Moscow–Volga Canal and Third Panzer Group had a bridgehead at Yakhroma the next morning; 2nd Panzer Division stood west of Krasnaya Polyana, roughly twelve miles north of the city[S11].

Proximity was not capacity. On the night of 28 November Bock committed his remaining army group reserve, Lehrbrigade 900 — in practice a battalion — to the drive on Moscow, and on 29 November warned that if the northern attack failed the battle would degenerate into a frontal struggle[S11]. The same day Stalin transferred First Shock, Twentieth and Tenth Armies to Zhukov for counter-attack[S11].

30 November to 1 December

On 30 November Zhukov sent Stalin the Western Front's plan: the northern blow towards Klin, Solnechnogorsk and Istra, the southern against the flank and rear of the grouping threatening Moscow from the Tula direction. The surviving document is the original, in Sokolovsky's hand, carrying autograph signatures including Stalin's and Zhukov's[S4].

It was not the beginning of Soviet counter-offensive planning — reserves had been gathering through November and First Shock and Twentieth Armies were being formed during it[S11]. But it was written while the Germans were still attacking, not after they had begun to fall back.

The next day Bock recorded that the condition of his troops was extremely strained and that continuing the advance carried the danger of a breakdown[S5]. Ziemke and Bauer record him telling the army commander-in-chief that Army Group Centre lacked the strength to encircle Moscow, let alone carry out the further operations still under discussion[S11].

The date is set

Also on 1 December, Stavka Directive No. 005292 ordered the Kalinin Front to prepare a counterstroke towards Mikulino-Gorodishche and Turginovo, to begin on 5 or 6 December depending on the readiness of the troops[S6].

German offensive operations had not stopped. On 1 December a Fourth Army formation broke through south of the Moscow–Smolensk highway. But by 3 December Fourth Panzer Group was reporting its offensive strength essentially exhausted, while Third Panzer Group fought First Shock Army around Yakhroma[S11].

On 4 December Western Front Directive No. 0047/op ordered the 20th, 16th and 5th Armies onto the offensive on the Istra axis[S7] — front-level attack orders issued before the general counter-offensive opened.

The temperature fell sharply on the night of 4–5 December. German weapons, vehicles and machinery failed and frostbite casualties rose[S11]. The cold was not incidental. It arrived at spearheads whose offensive power was already largely spent.

5 to 8 December

The counter-offensive was staggered. The Kalinin Front attacked on 5 December; the main Western and South-Western Front attacks followed on 6 December, with individual armies entering on their own timetables[S6][S11].

German expectations were mixed and mostly wrong. On 4 December Bock judged a Soviet counter-offensive unlikely, believing the new formations reported opposite him had simply been moved from quieter sectors[S11]. The following evening, at 20:25 on 5 December, Third Panzer Group headquarters sent Army Group Centre an assessment based on the situation at 15:00: Soviet pressure was mounting against its extended eastern front, and the danger was a decisive counter-offensive against the German northern flank[S8]. That is a report of an attack already developing, not a forecast.

By 7 December Bock was recording Third Panzer Group's deteriorating position, formations shifted to meet Soviet attacks, almost nothing available from neighbouring commands, and Soviet ability to bring in formations that German estimates had not anticipated[S9].

On 8 December Army Group Centre Order No. 2939 transferred Third Panzer Group to Fourth Army, postponed the offensive operation it had been assigned, and instructed the command to avoid an encirclement if one threatened[S10].

Eight days after Zhukov's memorandum, the battle had changed from a German attempt to encircle Moscow into a German attempt to keep an exposed formation from being trapped.

Disputed and mythologised

Overstated

General Winter defeated the Germans before Moscow.

Weather had major operational effects: October mud slowed movement severely, and the December cold caused frostbite, immobilised vehicles and interfered with German weapons[S11]. But Army Group Centre's own November plan treated the state of the ground as a precondition for resuming the advance, not as a threat to it[S3]. And Bock's warning of 1 December is about the condition of his troops, not the weather — a strain already present before the counter-offensive and before the hard frost[S5]. Hitler's directive of 8 December ending larger offensive operations attributed the decision to the early cold, though Ziemke and Bauer note the offensives had already stopped in practice[S11]. The explanation was available to the German command at the time, and convenient to it. Winter aggravated a crisis it did not create.

Myth

Siberian divisions arrived at the last moment and saved Moscow.

Eastern reinforcement was real, though its scale is disputed: Ziemke and Bauer count seventy divisions transferred from the Soviet Far East by 1 December with a further twenty-seven from Central Asia and the Transcaucasus, while later scholarship gives figures an order of magnitude smaller for the Far Eastern Military District specifically[S11]. But troops from the east had been appearing at the front since October, and they arrived alongside reserves drawn from other fronts, newly formed armies and formations raised in the Moscow region[S11]. On 4 December Bock thought some of the troops facing Third Panzer Group might be Siberian, while underestimating the Soviet capacity to field genuinely new formations[S11]. What the record shows is a broad and sustained mobilisation, not a single body of men arriving in time to turn the battle.

Unproven

The Germans were on the brink of taking Moscow.

The map invites it: German formations on the Moscow–Volga Canal, twelve miles from the city[S11]. Stahel argues the map position was deceptive, because the offensive strength of Third and Fourth Panzer Groups was approaching exhaustion and their operational mobility was badly compromised[S12]. That reading fits Bock's own warning of 1 December and the speed with which Army Group Centre moved from offensive planning to fear of encirclement between 5 and 8 December[S5][S8][S10]. Whether more men, better weather or another week would have carried the Germans into Moscow is a counterfactual, and nothing here settles it.

Gaps in the record

  • Translation. The German documents used here — the Typhoon and November plans, the Third Panzer Group telegram, Bock's order, and Bock's diary entries — are captured Wehrmacht material published in Russian collections and read in Russian translation. The German originals were not consulted, so wording that turns on translation cannot be checked against them[S1][S3][S5][S8][S9][S10].
  • When the counter-offensive actually began. The Soviet documents establish the Stavka timetable of 5–6 December and front-level orders from 4 December. They do not establish when every army and division began to move[S6][S7][S11].
  • Where the reinforcements came from. The totals for transfers from the east come from Ziemke and Bauer, and later scholarship gives substantially lower figures for the Far Eastern Military District. The primary Soviet documents used here do not trace individual formations from origin to the Moscow front, so the discrepancy is not resolved[S11].
  • Stahel's argument. Only the openly displayed Cambridge summary was readable. The chapter text is access-controlled and has not been treated as consulted[S12].
