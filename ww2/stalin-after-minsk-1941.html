Stalin After Minsk: The Two Days Missing from the Record

In brief

At the end of June 1941 the Soviet position in Belorussia collapsed. Western Front reports place German armour bypassing Minsk before 28 June, and by the afternoon of 29 June German tanks were moving east from the city towards Mogilev[S2][S3][S4].

The notebooks recording visitors received in Stalin's Kremlin office run through 28 June, contain no entries for 29 or 30 June, and resume on 1 July[S1]. That is a two-day gap in recorded office appointments. It is not a record of where Stalin was, whom he spoke to, or how he was.

On 30 June the State Defence Committee was created, with Stalin as chairman and the full powers of the state concentrated in its hands[S7]. Almost everything vivid that is told about the days in between — the retreat to the dacha, the frightened man who thought his colleagues had come to arrest him — reaches us through the Mikoyan memoir tradition, recorded decades later and not free of later editing[S8][S9].

What happened

The military collapse

By no later than 27 June, Western Front commander Dmitry Pavlov reported that a large German armoured column had passed the fortified area and was bypassing Minsk from the north-west, appearing in the Soviet rear[S2]. The General Staff's summary for the morning of 28 June described the Western Front still fighting as its formations withdrew[S3]. A 13th Army combat report of 29 June recorded German tanks coming out of Minsk at 16:00 and moving east along the Mogilev road[S4].

These are contemporaneous operational records. They establish the military situation in which the leadership crisis took place, not anything about how Stalin responded to it.

The gap

The principal contemporary source is the published edition of the notebooks kept by the office that registered people received by Stalin in the Kremlin, based on records held in RGASPI[S1]. It is a record of office receptions, not of his whereabouts.

Entries continue through 28 June. There are none for 29 or 30 June. The record resumes on 1 July[S1]. Mark Edele, working from the same published register, notes the two blank days and the resumption of meetings on the afternoon of 1 July[S9].

What that establishes is precise and limited: no visitors were registered as received in Stalin's Kremlin office on those two days. It does not establish that he had stopped working, cut himself off, remained at his dacha throughout, or was in any particular state. The notebooks record office receptions. They are not a diary of his location, his telephone calls, or his private meetings[S1][S9].

The government was demonstrably functioning, and Stalin's name is on its output. On 29 June the Council of People's Commissars and the Party Central Committee issued a directive to party and Soviet organisations in the front-line regions, acknowledging the loss of territory in Lithuania, Latvia, western Belorussia and western Ukraine, and ordering the mobilisation of resources and organised resistance. The published text ends with Stalin's name, as chairman of the Council of People's Commissars and secretary of the Central Committee, with Molotov as deputy chairman beneath him[S5]. That establishes formal responsibility rather than showing that he drafted it or was at his desk when it went out — but it is not the record of a man who had dropped out of government. The same day Molotov received the British ambassador, Stafford Cripps, to discuss British assistance[S6].

What Stalin did not do was speak. He made no public address until 3 July[S11]. That silence is part of why the story of a disappearance took hold.

What Mikoyan remembered

The detailed story of these days comes from testimony given long afterwards. In the account published by Georgii Kumanev, Mikoyan described a meeting of Stalin, Molotov, Malenkov, Beria and himself, Stalin's attempt to get information from Defence Commissar Semyon Timoshenko, and a visit to the People's Commissariat of Defence which ended badly — followed by Stalin's departure for his dacha at Kuntsevo[S8].

Mikoyan placed this on 29 June — one of the two days the register is blank[S8]. That looks like a contradiction and is not one. Edele keeps the 29 June date and suggests the meeting took place in Stalin's Kremlin apartment rather than his working office, which is precisely the distinction the reception notebooks draw[S9]. A blank office register and a meeting elsewhere in the Kremlin are compatible, and the register records no visit to the Defence Commissariat either way[S1].

Edele treats Mikoyan as the principal eyewitness for these two days while warning that retrospective recollection is not reliably exact on chronology[S9]. He accepts that Stalin underwent a short crisis of confidence, and notes that historians have read the episode variously: as a genuine breakdown, as behaviour of no special significance, or as a calculated manoeuvre[S9].

The State Defence Committee

For 30 June a contemporary document survives. The Politburo resolution, in Georgy Malenkov's hand, established the State Defence Committee with Stalin as chairman, Molotov as deputy chairman, and Kliment Voroshilov, Malenkov and Lavrentiy Beria as members. It concentrated the fullness of state power in the committee and required all party, state, economic and military bodies, and all citizens, to comply with its decisions[S7]. The formal establishing act was issued jointly in the names of the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet, the Council of People's Commissars and the Central Committee[S7].

What the resolution does not preserve is the discussion that produced it: who proposed the committee, what was said, why particular men were included, or how Stalin responded when it was put to him[S7].

For that, the account again rests on testimony. The published Mikoyan account has Stalin's colleagues meeting without him, agreeing on an emergency governing body, and going to Kuntsevo to ask him to head it[S8]. Edele's reconstruction has Molotov taking the initiative on 30 June, meeting Malenkov, Voroshilov and Beria, with Mikoyan and Nikolai Voznesensky joining them, before the group travelled to the dacha[S9]. John Barber and Mark Harrison describe Stalin as momentarily shaken and his associates as restoring the functioning of the leadership by building the new committee around him — the first major step in constructing the wartime system of government[S10].

Both accounts agree that something more than an interruption of routine occurred. Both are interpretations assembled from the administrative record and later recollection. Neither converts Mikoyan's memory into a contemporaneous source[S8][S9][S10].

Disputed and mythologised

Myth

Stalin vanished for a week when the invasion came.

The published register shows him receiving visitors in his Kremlin office through 28 June, then two days with no entries, then meetings resuming on 1 July[S1]. Whatever happened at Kuntsevo, the documented interruption is two days at the end of June — not the week that often appears in the retellings, and not the opening days of the war, when the same register shows him working[S1].

Overstated

Stalin abandoned the government for those two days.

The gap in the register is real, and both Edele and Barber and Harrison treat the episode as a genuine short crisis[S9][S10]. But the register shows only an absence of recorded Kremlin office appointments[S1]. The party-government directive of 29 June went out over Stalin's name as chairman of the Council of People's Commissars and secretary of the Central Committee[S5]. That does not prove he wrote it, or that he was working normally — but total abandonment is harder to sustain than the retellings allow.

Unproven

Stalin thought his colleagues had come to arrest him.

This is the most quoted detail of the whole episode and the least securely sourced. It appears in the published Mikoyan text[S8], but Edele points to insertions made by Anastas Mikoyan's son Sergo into his father's account, and attributes the arrest interpretation to Sergo and to Beria rather than to Anastas himself. Edele's own position is that whether Stalin actually expected arrest is unknowable[S9]. No contemporaneous record of the conversation at Kuntsevo appears in these sources, and the GKO resolution says nothing about it[S7].

Gaps in the record

These are limits of the documents used here.

  • What the register is. It records who was registered as received in Stalin's Kremlin office. It is not a continuous account of his movements, private meetings, telephone calls, or anything at Kuntsevo — and, as Edele's reading shows, not necessarily of meetings elsewhere in the Kremlin either[S1][S9].
  • The Mikoyan text. The published account has passed through later editing by Sergo Mikoyan. Which details are Anastas Mikoyan's own and which are his son's is not resolved here[S8][S9].
  • The dacha meeting. No contemporaneous transcript or minute of the conversation at which the State Defence Committee was reportedly proposed appears in these sources[S7][S8][S9].
  • How the GKO was decided. The resolution establishes date, membership, chairmanship and powers, and nothing about the deliberation behind it — including whether Molotov first proposed it[S7].
  • What reached Stalin, and when. The military reports show the collapse around Minsk as Soviet commands understood it. They do not establish which reports reached Stalin at which moment[S2][S3][S4].
