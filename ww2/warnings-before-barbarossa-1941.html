Before Barbarossa: The Warnings Stalin Received

In brief

The Soviet leadership was not short of warnings before Germany attacked on 22 June 1941. The United States passed one to the Soviet ambassador in March. Soviet military intelligence was assessing German attack scenarios by the same month. On 17 June a report addressed directly to Stalin said German preparations were complete and the blow could come at any time[S2][S7][S8].

What the documents show is not a leadership in ignorance, but warnings arriving from many directions, in conflicting terms, and assessed unevenly. Days before the invasion the Soviet government was still publicly dismissing invasion rumours as baseless. Yet on 21 June the high command told border districts an attack might come within forty-eight hours — while warning them, in the same order, not to be provoked[S5][S9].

What happened

The German plan

Hitler's Directive No. 21, issued on 18 December 1940, ordered the Wehrmacht to prepare to crush the Soviet Union in a rapid campaign. It required longer-term preparations to be completed by 15 May 1941 and described concealment of the intention to attack as decisive[S1].

The American warnings

By March the United States was trying to warn Moscow. On 1 March Secretary of State Cordell Hull instructed the American ambassador, Laurence Steinhardt, to seek an urgent meeting with Molotov and tell him confidentially that Washington held information it regarded as authentic indicating Germany intended to attack the Soviet Union in the not-distant future. Steinhardt was authorised to approach Vyshinsky if he could not reach Molotov[S2].

That approach was cancelled because the warning had already gone through another channel. Hull's telegram of 4 March recorded that Under Secretary of State Sumner Welles had conveyed the information to the Soviet ambassador in Washington on 1 March. The evidence establishes that an official Soviet representative received the American warning — not that Stalin personally did[S3].

The warning stayed live in the exchanges that followed. On 20 March the Soviet ambassador asked Welles whether the United States had further information confirming what he had been told in confidence. Welles replied that additional information confirmed the earlier report[S4].

What Soviet intelligence was assessing

On the same day, Lieutenant-General Filipp Golikov, head of Red Army military intelligence, submitted an assessment to the People's Commissariat of Defence, the Council of People's Commissars and the Party Central Committee concerning possible German operations against the Soviet Union[S7].

The report is the clearest surviving picture of how the warnings looked from inside the system. Golikov listed numerous reported scenarios and dates, including May and the period from 15 May to 15 June. His conclusion treated reports of an inevitable spring war as possible British or German disinformation, and the opening section was suspicious of material sourced through Anglo-American channels. He judged that the most likely moment for action against the Soviet Union would come after a German victory over Britain, or a peace settlement favourable to Germany[S7].

David Murphy reads Golikov's handling of intelligence as consistently emphasising German operations against Britain, reinforcing Stalin's belief that Hitler would not attack in the east until Britain was beaten. That interpretation rests on excerpts from Murphy's book rather than the complete text[S13].

What the agents reported

The intelligence that reached Moscow in the final weeks did not converge on a single date. On 1 June the Tokyo station under Richard Sorge, reporting as "Ramzay", said an attack would come in the second half of June. On 17 June a further Sorge report indicated that the German move had been put off until the end of the month[S15][S16].

On 20 June — two days before the invasion — an agent reporting as "Kosta" cabled from Sofia that war would begin on 21 or 22 June, with information on the disposition of German divisions[S14].

The public position

By mid-June the Soviet government's public line remained reassuring. A TASS communiqué broadcast on 13 June dismissed reports that Germany intended to break the pact and attack as being without foundation. It said German troop movements eastward should be attributed to other motives, and described Soviet reservist call-ups and forthcoming manoeuvres as routine training rather than preparation for war[S5].

17 June

The same day Sorge was reporting a postponement, People's Commissar for State Security Vsevolod Merkulov sent Stalin, Molotov and Beria an agent report based on information from Berlin. A source connected to the German air ministry reported that preparations for an armed attack on the Soviet Union had been completed and that the blow could be expected at any time. The report also carried information on intended bombing targets and on appointments to the military-economic administrations intended for occupied Soviet territory[S8].

Murphy reproduces what he identifies as a handwritten response by Stalin to Merkulov, dismissing the air ministry source as a disinformer. It is among the most quoted lines in the whole subject, and its evidential position is weak: the published archival copy of the report does not show any such annotation, and Geoffrey Roberts records doubt about the authenticity of the quotation. It should not be read as documentary fact on the same footing as the report it supposedly annotates[S8][S11][S13].

The last days

By 21 June Soviet concern had sharpened. Molotov met the German ambassador, Friedrich-Werner von der Schulenburg, and raised repeated violations of Soviet airspace, an unanswered Soviet protest, rumours of imminent war, and departures from the German embassy. Schulenburg said Berlin had not informed him sufficiently and that he could not answer Molotov's questions[S10].

Later that day, Defence Commissar Semyon Timoshenko and Chief of the General Staff Georgy Zhukov issued a directive to the border military districts warning that a surprise German attack might occur on 22–23 June. It ordered forces to full combat readiness, firing positions in the fortified regions to be occupied secretly, aircraft dispersed and camouflaged, air defences alerted. In the same document, commanders were warned not to succumb to provocative actions that might produce major complications[S9].

Germany attacked in the early hours of 22 June.

Stalin's account

Stalin explained his pre-war judgement six weeks later, during Harry Hopkins's visit to Moscow on 31 July. Hopkins recorded Stalin saying the Red Army had faced a surprise attack, and that he himself had believed Hitler would not strike. Stalin also maintained that he had taken every possible precaution to mobilise the army. This is his retrospective account, given after the disaster had begun — evidence of how he chose to explain his earlier judgement, not independent proof of what was done before 22 June[S6].

Disputed and mythologised

Overstated

Stalin was handed the date of 22 June and ignored it.

Exact-date intelligence does exist in the surviving record, but not in the form the story requires. The agent "Kosta" named 21 or 22 June — in a report sent on 20 June, two days beforehand[S14]. Sorge's messages moved the other way: an attack in the second half of June on 1 June, then a postponement to the end of the month on 17 June[S15][S16]. The March American warning said only that an attack was intended in the not-distant future[S2]. No document here shows all three elements together: the correct date, substantial advance notice, and receipt by Stalin personally.

Unproven

Stalin's contemptuous note dismissing the 17 June warning.

Widely quoted, and not on the document. The published archival copy of the Merkulov report carries no such annotation, and Roberts records doubt about the quotation's authenticity. It survives through Murphy's account rather than through the record[S8][S11][S13].

Myth

Schulenburg secretly warned the Soviet ambassador on 5 May that the invasion was coming.

Geoffrey Roberts traces this to an article by G. Kumanev published in Pravda on 22 June 1989, based on Anastas Mikoyan's recollections. Roberts notes that Dekanozov's own diary report of the 5 May meeting records Schulenburg expressing concern about relations between the two countries, and no warning of an attack[S11].

Unproven

Stalin watched European mutton prices to see if Hitler was coming.

The anecdote appears in later accounts of Soviet intelligence practice, usually linking expected German demand for winter sheepskins to movements in the European mutton market. No primary documentary support for it has been found, and Golikov's detailed March report — which shows exactly how Soviet intelligence was reading German intentions — says nothing about sheepskins or mutton[S7].

Overstated

Stalin trusted Hitler and believed the pact would hold.

Stalin told Hopkins he had believed Hitler would not strike, which establishes an expectation about that moment rather than settled trust or confidence that war would never come[S6]. Golikov's March report was already analysing possible German operations against the Soviet Union, so the possibility of war was inside Soviet planning months earlier[S7].

Myth

No military precautions were taken before the invasion.

The 21 June directive ordered readiness, dispersal, camouflage and defensive measures precisely because a surprise attack was thought possible on 22–23 June[S9]. Whether those orders came early enough, and how far they had been carried out by the time the attack began, the document does not say.

Gaps in the record

These are limits of the documents used here. The Soviet intelligence archives contain many more reports from April to June 1941 than one article can carry, and a fuller reconstruction of the intelligence chain is possible from them.

  • How the American warning travelled. The documents do not establish the route from the Soviet ambassador in Washington to Moscow, or whether Stalin read it. The State Department's own edition notes that no separate record of Welles's 1 March conversation has been found[S3].
  • Churchill's April warning. No openly accessible primary documentation for it appears here. Gorodetsky's article cites the relevant British archival files, but its text was not accessible, so those references are leads rather than evidence of what the warning said or what it achieved[S12].
  • Whether Stalin saw particular reports. The Sorge and "Kosta" messages survive, so what was reported and when is established. What is not established for any of them is the route onward, or whether Stalin read them[S14][S15][S16].
  • Stalin's marginal note. The published archival copy of the 17 June report does not show it, and its authenticity is questioned[S8][S11][S13].
  • Who wrote the TASS communiqué. The text establishes what was said publicly on 13–14 June, not who drafted it, who approved the wording, or what it was meant to achieve[S5].
  • Stalin's part in the 21 June directive. The document establishes the orders issued under Timoshenko's and Zhukov's names, not Stalin's role in the decision, when individual commands received it, or how far it had been implemented[S9].
