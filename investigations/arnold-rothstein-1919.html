Arnold Rothstein and the 1919 World Series: What the Record Shows

In brief

In the autumn of 1920 a Cook County grand jury investigating baseball scandals summoned Arnold Rothstein, the New York gambler whose name had already been publicly attached to the fixing of the 1919 World Series[S11][S12]. He appeared, told the jury he had been approached and had refused, and returned to New York. Thirteen people were indicted; Rothstein was not among them[S1].

At the 1921 criminal trial in Chicago, prosecution witness Bill "Sleepy Bill" Burns testified that Rothstein had in fact been among the organisers of the fix, and described specific payment terms[S2][S3]. The players and gamblers on trial were acquitted; the following morning Commissioner Kenesaw Mountain Landis banned them from professional baseball for life regardless of the verdict[S4][S5][S6].

What the contemporaneous record establishes is that Rothstein was named, subpoenaed, questioned and denied involvement in 1920; that he was not indicted; and that a witness under oath named him as an organiser eight months later. What it does not establish is which of those two accounts was true. No adjudication of Rothstein's role appears in the sources retrieved here, and none of the claims later made about a formal prosecutorial "exoneration" of him is supported by a primary document in this pack[S1][S2][S3][S8].

What happened

The grand jury opens, September 1920

Late in September 1920 the Cook County grand jury moved to investigate baseball scandals, with contemporaneous reporting explicitly referencing the 1919 World Series[S11]. In the same week, the sportswriter W. S. Farnsworth was reported as saying that Rothstein "holds the key" to the scandal; the same report noted that Rothstein had been summoned to appear before the grand jury, with some doubt expressed as to whether he would[S11]. This is a press characterisation and a report of a subpoena, not a finding — but it establishes that Rothstein's name was attached to the case publicly before he had given any account of himself.

27 October 1920: an appearance, a denial, and no indictment

Wire-service reporting from late October 1920 records that Rothstein appeared before the Chicago grand jury and then returned to New York. As reported, he told the jury that Abe Attell and Bill Burns had approached him with a plan to "buy out" the White Sox for $100,000, that he had refused, and that he "threw the men out of his office"[S1]. This is Rothstein's own account of himself, as relayed by the press — not a transcript, and not a finding by the jury.

The same reporting lists thirteen people indicted at that stage: eight White Sox players and a group of gamblers including Abe Attell, J. J. "Sport" Sullivan and a figure named "Rachel Brown". Rothstein does not appear on that list[S1]. Non-indictment is a documented fact of the October 1920 presentments as reported; it is a narrower thing than an exoneration, a distinction the record does not blur even where later summaries have.

Burns names names, July 1921

The criminal trial opened in Chicago in the summer of 1921. On 19–20 July, Associated Press wire copy carried in at least two papers reported that Burns, testifying for the prosecution, named Arnold Rothstein, Abe Attell, David Zelcer, Billy Maharg and himself among those who organised the conspiracy. He described payment terms of $20,000 to the players after each game lost, and quoted Eddie Cicotte on the throwing of the first game[S2][S3]. Both printings derive from the same wire report rather than independent reporting, so they corroborate the wording of what Burns said without adding a second, separate witness to it.

The same wire coverage also reported Rothstein's earlier position: that he had testified before the grand jury the previous autumn that he had been approached but had refused, and that his name had been used by Attell without his permission[S2][S3]. The trial thus put a sworn accusation and a previously reported denial into the same record, without the sources here showing that either was tested against the other in that courtroom.

Acquittal and Landis's ban, August 1921

The jury acquitted. The morning after the verdicts, newspapers carried the statement of Kenesaw Mountain Landis, then serving as Commissioner of Baseball, announcing lifetime bans regardless of the outcome in court: "Regardless of the verdict of juries, no player that throws a ball game, no player that undertakes or promises to throw a ball game, no player that sits in a conference with a bunch of crooked players and gamblers where the ways and means of throwing a game are discussed and does not promptly tell his club about it, will ever play professional baseball"[S4]. A second outlet the following day printed substantially the same wording, confirming the language across two contemporaneous sources[S5]. A third report, from a different wire service, described the immediate enforcement of the bans by both Landis and White Sox owner Charles Comiskey[S6].

Rothstein was not a party to this trial and was not among those banned by Landis's statement, which addressed players who had sat in conference with gamblers, not the gamblers themselves[S4][S6].

The stolen confessions

Coverage of the same day's events in another outlet, via the Associated Press, added a detail bearing on how the acquittal should be read: "Three of the players confessed to the grand jury last fall but the confessions were stolen from the court records"[S13]. This is a contemporaneous press claim about the disappearance of evidence before trial. No documentation of the alleged theft — who removed the confessions, when, or how it came to be publicly known — was retrieved in this pack.

Rachel Brown, and the edges of the archive

Among the thirteen people indicted in October 1920 was a gambler identified as "Rachel Brown", named alongside Attell and Sullivan[S1]. Later research has treated the identity behind that name as an open question, canvassing several possibilities without resolving it[S9].

A separate line of institutional description points to further primary material never brought into this account: the National Baseball Hall of Fame's collection guide lists trial exhibits, a Hotel Sinton register said to show the registrations of White Sox players together with Attell and other gamblers, and photostats of checks endorsed by Attell[S7]. That guide describes holdings; it is not itself the evidence, and none of the underlying items was retrieved for this article.

Disputed and mythologised

Unproven

Arnold Rothstein financed the fixing of the 1919 World Series.

This is the accusation on which Rothstein's later reputation largely rests, and it has direct sworn support: Bill Burns testified at the 1921 trial that Rothstein was among those who organised the conspiracy, and set out specific payment terms[S2][S3]. Set against that is Rothstein's own account, given to the Chicago grand jury the previous autumn as reported by the press: that Attell and Burns had approached him and he had refused, and that his name had been used without his permission[S1][S2][S3].

He was not among the thirteen people indicted in October 1920[S1], and no charge against him was tried at the 1921 proceedings, so no verdict — by acquittal or conviction — was ever returned on the question. What the sources retrieved here show is an accusation under oath and a denial reported by the press; they do not show which was correct, and nothing here adjudicates it either way.

Overstated

Chicago prosecutors publicly exonerated Rothstein.

A later secondary summary states that Rothstein "strongly denied any involvement" and that "Chicago prosecutors publicly exonerated him"[S8]. The first half is supported directly by the contemporaneous reporting of his grand-jury appearance[S1]. The second half goes further than the primary reporting retrieved here shows. What the October 1920 press coverage establishes is that he was not among those indicted[S1] — a prosecutorial decision not to charge is not the same thing as a public statement of exoneration, and no such statement appears in the newspaper reporting used for this article.

Overstated

The 1921 acquittals proved the Black Sox players were innocent.

The players and gamblers charged were acquitted, and that verdict is well documented[S4][S5][S6]. But Landis's statement, issued the same morning, treated the criminal verdict as irrelevant to the players' standing in the sport: bans followed "regardless of the verdict of juries"[S4][S5]. Contemporaneous reporting also recorded that confessions reportedly given by three players to the grand jury the previous autumn had gone missing from the court record before the trial[S13] — a circumstance that bears directly on what an acquittal at that trial can be read to have decided. An acquittal establishes that guilt was not proved beyond reasonable doubt on the charges actually tried. Neither the contemporaneous press nor baseball's own commissioner treated it as an independent finding of innocence.

Unproven

"Rachel Brown" was one of Rothstein's aliases.

The gambler indicted alongside Attell and Sullivan as "Rachel Brown" has an identity that later research treats as genuinely unresolved, exploring several possibilities without settling on one[S9]. Nothing in the sources retrieved here links that name to Rothstein specifically, and nothing establishes any other identification either. The question remains open on the documents available, not answered in either direction.

Gaps in the record

  • The grand jury record. No certified transcript or indictment text from the 1920 Cook County proceedings was retrieved for this article. Rothstein's appearance, his testimony, and the list of those indicted are known here only through contemporaneous newspaper wire reporting[S1][S11][S12].
  • The trial transcript. Burns's 1921 testimony is known here only through Associated Press wire summaries printed in two papers on the same day, not a verbatim court transcript. The precise wording, and any cross-examination of Burns on the point, are not available from these sources[S2][S3].
  • The exoneration claim. A secondary account states that prosecutors publicly exonerated Rothstein. No primary document recording such a statement was located in this pack, so the claim rests on a later summary rather than a document that could be checked directly[S8].
  • The archive exhibits. The National Baseball Hall of Fame's finding aid describes a Hotel Sinton register and photostat checks connecting players and gamblers to the scandal. These are institutional descriptions of holdings; the underlying items themselves were not retrieved and could not be examined for this article[S7].
  • The stolen confessions. A single contemporaneous report states that three players' grand-jury confessions disappeared from the court record before trial. No documentation of the theft — its date, its circumstances, or who was responsible — was retrieved here[S13].
