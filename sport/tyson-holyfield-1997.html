Tyson v Holyfield II, 1997: What the Disqualification Established

In brief

On 28 June 1997, at the MGM Grand Garden in Las Vegas, Mike Tyson challenged Evander Holyfield for the WBA heavyweight title in their second meeting. In the third round Tyson bit Holyfield's right ear during a clinch; referee Mills Lane deducted two points and allowed the bout to continue after ringside physician Flip Homansky examined Holyfield. Before the round ended Tyson bit Holyfield's left ear as well, and Lane disqualified him, leaving Holyfield champion[S1][S2][S3][S4][S15].

Nevada State Athletic Commission executive director Marc Ratner announced an immediate suspension and withheld Tyson's reported $30 million purse pending a hearing. On 9 July 1997 the Commission unanimously revoked Tyson's licence and fined him the statutory maximum, reported as $3,000,000 by two papers and "almost $3 million" by a third, with a right to reapply after a year. Fifteen months later, on 19 October 1998, the Commission voted 4–1 to restore his licence following a public hearing and medical evaluation[S2][S5][S6][S7][S8][S9][S12][S13].

What the contemporaneous record establishes is the sequence inside the ring — two bites, a point deduction, a disqualification — together with the regulatory response that followed: suspension, hearing, revocation, fine and reinstatement. The record documents two bites, a point deduction and a disqualification, and the regulatory response. It also records that a fragment reached the hospital but was not viable, and the surgeon described the loss as roughly one inch by one-half inch[S1][S5][S10][S11][S6][S8][S9].

What happened

28 June: two bites in round three

Holyfield defended his WBA heavyweight title against Tyson at the MGM Grand Garden. In the third round, accounts differ on Tyson's mouthpiece at the first bite: the Los Angeles Times reports he spat it out just before biting Holyfield's right ear, while the Washington Post says he was still wearing it, drawing blood[S4][S2]. Lane called a halt and ringside physician Flip Homansky examined Holyfield before the fight was allowed to continue[S2][S3][S4].

Lane deducted two points, but the accounts of how they were allocated differ slightly. The Los Angeles Times' chronology, supported by a companion report, records one point for the bite and a second for Tyson pushing Holyfield during the stoppage; the Washington Post's same-night report simply states that "Lane took two points from Tyson" without breaking down the second[S1][S4][S10]. The fight resumed after the examination[S2][S4].

The second bite and disqualification

Later in the same round Tyson again spat out his mouthpiece and bit Holyfield's left ear[S2][S4]. Ringside judge Duane Ford, describing the earlier bite, was quoted afterwards saying Tyson "bit a hunk out of his right ear and spit it on the floor"[S2]. At the end of the round, after consulting NSAC executive director Marc Ratner, Lane disqualified Tyson[S4][S2]. Asked on camera by broadcaster Jim Gray to explain the ruling, Lane said: "I disqualified Tyson because of the second foul"[S2]. Holyfield retained his WBA title[S1][S15].

The ear fragment

A piece of Holyfield's right ear was found in the ring. The Washington Post, citing the treating plastic surgeon, reported it reached Valley Hospital but was not viable; a Los Angeles Times piece reported it was lost en route[S5][S11]. The accounts conflict, but the surgeon said the fragment that arrived could not be used. The treating plastic surgeon described the loss as "a one-inch by one-half-inch" piece; earlier accounts used less precise terms like "hunk" or a ringside "half-inch" estimate[S5][S10][S2]. Holyfield was sent for plastic surgery regardless of which account of the fragment is correct[S1][S10].

29 June: suspension and purse withheld

Immediately after the fight, Ratner announced that Tyson was temporarily suspended and that his purse, reported at $30 million, would be withheld pending a disciplinary hearing[S2][S5][S10]. NSAC chairman Elias Ghanem confirmed the suspension and said a hearing would follow the next Tuesday[S10]. Separately, the Nevada Athletic Commission and Las Vegas police were reported to be reviewing Tyson's conduct, including a melee that followed the decision[S5].

9 July 1997: revocation and fine

The Commission's disciplinary hearing was held before commissioners Elias Ghanem, James Nave, Lorenzo Fertitta, Luther Mack and Nat Carasali; Tyson did not attend, according to his attorney[S6][S8]. Nevada law capped the maximum fine at 10 percent of a boxer's purse — for Tyson, close to $3 million on a purse of nearly $30 million[S6]. The Commission voted unanimously to revoke Tyson's licence and impose that maximum fine, reported as exactly $3,000,000 by the Los Angeles Times and the San Francisco Chronicle, and as "almost $3 million" by the Washington Post[S7][S8][S9]. Deputy Attorney General Joe Rolston said Tyson could apply to be relicensed after one year, and could be refused annually thereafter; a recently passed federal law was reported at the time as likely to lead other states to honor Nevada's action, though a next-day report noted the law did not specifically address revocations and said other states were expected to honor the spirit of the ban[S8][S9][S6][S7].

19 October 1998: reinstatement

After a public hearing that included medical testimony, the Commission voted 4–1 on 19 October 1998 to restore Tyson's licence. Chairman Elias Ghanem administered an admonition and the reinstatement was reported as conditioned on continuing therapy[S12][S13]. Other states, which had honoured Nevada's 1997 revocation, were then expected to recognise the restored licence[S12].

Disputed and mythologised

Myth

Tyson's boxing licence was revoked for life.

This phrasing appears on an ESPN-hosted transcript page from October 1998, in the context of Tyson's medical evaluation before reinstatement[S14]. It sits against a year of contemporaneous reporting that describes a revocable sanction: Tyson could reapply for a licence after one year, and be denied annually thereafter if refused[S6][S8][S9]. The Commission did in fact reinstate him within fifteen months, on 19 October 1998[S12][S13]. The record does not support a lifetime ban; the ESPN phrasing reads as a later shorthand for a sanction that was, from the outset, conditional and reviewable.

Overstated

Tyson lost his $30 million purse for biting Holyfield.

Ratner withheld the reported $30 million purse immediately after the fight, pending a hearing[S2][S5][S10]. What the Commission ultimately imposed, on 9 July, was a fine equal to the statutory maximum of 10 percent of that purse — reported as $3,000,000, or "almost $3 million"[S6][S7][S8][S9]. Nothing retrieved here states what became of the remaining roughly $27 million once the temporary hold was resolved. The popular claim that Tyson "lost his purse" conflates the initial withholding with the much smaller fine that actually followed.

Overstated

Mills Lane disqualified Tyson entirely on his own initiative.

In a courtside interview moments after the ruling, Lane told broadcaster Jim Gray, "I disqualified Tyson because of the second foul" — a remark often repeated as evidence the referee acted alone, mid-ring, on his own judgement[S2]. The Los Angeles Times' chronology of the fight records that Lane in fact consulted NSAC executive director Marc Ratner before delivering the disqualification at the end of the round[S4]. The two accounts are not necessarily incompatible — a referee's on-camera explanation of his reasoning is not the same thing as a full account of who he spoke to first — but the record does not support treating Lane's own quoted words as the complete story of how the decision was reached.

Myth

The bitten-off piece of Holyfield's ear was recovered and used in his surgery.

The treating plastic surgeon said the fragment that reached the hospital was not viable and was not used; the ear was stitched, with later reconstruction planned using a cartilage graft[S5].

Gaps in the record

  • The ear fragment. Conflicting reports exist on the chain of custody, but the treating plastic surgeon said a fragment reached Valley Hospital and was not viable[S1][S11][S5].
  • The measurement. The treating plastic surgeon described the loss as "one-inch by one-half-inch"; earlier reports also used less precise descriptions[S5][S10].
  • The post-fight melee. Police were reported to be investigating the disorder that followed the decision. Whether any charge was ultimately filed does not appear in the sources retrieved for this article[S5].
  • The Commission's own records. No NSAC written disciplinary order for the 9 July 1997 revocation, nor a written reinstatement decision for 19 October 1998, was retrieved. Their existence and substance are known here only through newspaper reporting on the hearings themselves[S6][S8][S9][S12][S13].
  • The purse accounting. Reports agree the purse was reported at about $30 million and the fine at $3 million, but none retrieved here gives an exact figure to the cent, nor states what became of the balance once the temporary withholding ended[S2][S5][S8][S9].
